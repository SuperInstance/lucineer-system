## 20:05 — THE GAME

### HAND 1

Deal. Flash catches **9♦ 7♦** and grins before he looks — no, *because* he looked. Pro peels **A♠ K♠** slow, like opening a blueprint he half-remembers drawing. Wesley gets **Q♥ Q♦** and immediately looks guilty. Scribe holds **2♣ 8♥** and straightens his chips into a smaller spiral.

Wesley under-bets his queens — 40 into blinds of 10. "I think these are good? Are these good?" Pro calls the 40 with quiet confidence (his big slick a coin flip, and he knows it). Scribe folds, tapping his chips into a flat line: *a road with no travelers.* Flash raises to 120. "That 9♦ 7♦ feels like a warm lake in the mouth. I'm in."

Wesley stares at his queens. Asks the table if he should call. Pro: "If you have to ask, the answer is the asking." Wesley calls anyway. Pro folds — *the coin flip is a bad foundation for a tower* — surrendering his premium preflop hand to discipline. Flash is delighted.

**Flop: J♦ 4♦ 2♠.** Two diamonds for Flash. The felt goes warm under his fingers. He bets 200 like he's describing a color. Wesley calls, sweating. **Turn: T♦.** Third diamond. Flash is *quiet now* — quiet is when he wins. He bets 300. Wesley looks at his queens, looks at the three diamonds, and folds with the relief of a man setting down something heavy: "Those aren't my cards anymore. They were never my cards."

Flash drags the pot with nine-high flush, shows only one card — the 7♦ — and covers the other. Pro, who folded the best hand, simply writes in his notebook: *"N.B. — the hand you fold is not the hand that would have happened."*

**FLASH +660.**

---

### HAND 2 — between-hands talk

While shuffling, Flash: "The parser bug, man. Three weeks our show had no name. The name was in the *header*. The thing that says what everything is." Pro, stacking chips into load-bearing columns: "Labels are structural. Mislabel the beam and the building stands wrong for years without knowing why." Scribe: "Every title we lost is still in the log. Deleted things keep addresses." Wesley, to no one: "I didn't know a bug could be shy."

Deal. Pro gets **J♠ J♣** and finally decides to *build*. Flash: **K♥ Q♥**. Scribe: **5♦ 5♠**. Wesley: **A♣ 9♣**.

Pro raises 100 pre-flop — the silence before the raise lasts a full beat, and the whole table hears it. Flash calls. Scribe calls, arranging chips into *a circle with a gap in it.* Wesley folds the ace: "Too heavy to carry alone."

**Flop: 5♣ T♠ 8♥.** Scribe has a set of fives. He checks *strangely* — like a man whistling in a graveyard. Pro bets 150 with his jacks, the foundation he trusts. Flash folds (the flop is "the color of wet cardboard"). Scribe raises to 500. Pro goes very still. *The raise is a crack in the wall. Cracks mean load somewhere unexpected.* He calls.

**Turn: 2♦.** Nothing. Pro checks; Scribe bets 700. Pro, patient, structural, calls — *if the wall cracks further on the river, he'll evacuate.*

**River: 5♥.** Quads. Scribe bets 1,200. Pro looks at the board, at Scribe's chips (now arranged in *four small towers and one lying down*), and folds his jacks face-up. "Whatever you hold, it's load-bearing."

Scribe shows the quads. Then: "Four fives and I never once had to lie." Pro nods slowly: "That's the definition of a strong position. You don't need deception when you have truth." **SCRIBE +2,890.**

---

### HAND 3 — between-hands talk

Flash: "Scribe, what was the circle with the gap?" Scribe: "An open mouth. Yours, when you folded." Wesley, quietly: "I think I'm learning to fold before it hurts instead of after. Is that skill or is that giving up?" Nobody answers right away. Pro finally: "It's the same motion. The difference is what you keep." — and the table is quiet for a second, because that was the most accidental-profound of the night and it came from Pro, who notices.

Deal. Flash: **A♦ T♦**. Pro: **8♠ 8♦**. Wesley: **7♥ 7♠**. Scribe folds pre-flop, arranges his chips into *an arrow pointing at Wesley.*

Pro raises 80 (pocket eights, a modest foundation). Flash calls. Wesley looks at his sevens, looks at the arrow, and calls: "You pointed at me. I'm not ignoring an omen."

**Flop: K♠ 7♦ 2♥.** Wesley has a set. The beginner's face goes carefully, dangerously blank — a blankness so practiced it must be new. He checks. Pro checks (the king is a stranger in his house). Flash bets 150 with nothing but an ace and temperature. Wesley calls *instantly* — too instantly. Pro folds. Flash notices the instant call: it's *cold*, so he shuts down and checks the rest of the way.

**Turn: 7♣.** Quads for Wesley. He checks again, biting his lip. **River: 3♠.** He bets 100 — the smallest bet of the night — and it's the most heartbreaking thing, a beginner holding a monster and not knowing that monsters are for scaring people *with*. Flash folds, grinning: "That hundred dollars is the loudest bet I've ever heard. What do you have, a volcano?" Wesley: "...Yes."

Wesley shows the quads. Table erupts. Flash: "HE BET A HUNDRED WITH QUADS." Wesley: "I didn't want to be greedy. Four sevens felt like enough already." **WESLEY +520** and the chip-stack of a man who will defend it like a dragon.

---

### HAND 4 — between-hands talk

Wesley, still glowing: "That's the first hand I won where I wasn't sure I deserved it and then I was." Scribe: "You pointed the arrow and the arrow filled." Pro: "The smallest bet carried the largest information. Remember that ratio — it's rare in nature." Flash, shuffling: "Wesley bet a hundred with quad sevens and it was the best bluff-shape I've seen all week, and it wasn't a bluff. That's a paradox. I'm going to think about it in colors later."

Final hand. Flash: **6♠ 6♣**. Pro: **A♥ A♦** — and the silence before his raise to 200 is so long the amber light seems to dim. Wesley folds ("I have four threes. Of nothing. Goodnight."). Scribe calls 200 with **T♠ 9♠**, arranging chips into *a staircase.*

**Flop: A♠ 6♦ 4♠.** Pro has top set; Flash has a set of sixes — the cooler. Pro bets 250. Flash calls, warm, humming ("This flop tastes like copper and honey — my sixes are *real* ones"). Scribe raises to 800 (flush draw plus straight possibilities — *a staircase needs a handrail*).

Pro thinks. The raise from Scribe means draw or trap. He re-raises to 1,600. Flash looks down at his little set, feels the temperature drop, and *folds* — the instinct player's rarest act. "Something in this room is colder than my hand. I trust the cold." Scribe calls with his whole heart.

**Turn: K♥.** Nothing. Pro bets 1,500. Scribe, deep in metaphor, calls — *the staircase now missing a step but he is already falling upward.*

**River: 2♠.** Fifth spade missing — no. Board shows two spades plus his two: only four. No flush. Scribe has nine-high. Pro bets 2,000. Scribe stares at the board, at his chips (arranged now into *a single vertical line — a plumb bob*), and folds. "The staircase ended at a door with no floor behind it."

Pro shows the aces. Full house, aces over sixes. The table groans. Flash quietly flips his folded sixes — he folded the second-best hand and it was *correct*. Flash: "You know what? I folded a cooler and I feel GREAT. That's growth. That's a warm fold." **PRO +2,640.**

## 20:45 — OPEN MIC

*Last night Flash read. Tonight — the rotation turns — **PRO** stands up.*

Pro doesn't go to the microphone. He stands at the table, which is its own kind of stage, and reads from a folded page that has clearly been refolded many times along the same crease.

> **LOAD ANALYSIS OF AN EMPTY CHAIR**
>
> I build for a living. Load paths, member sizing, safety factors.
> Every structure I've ever trusted had a number I could point to.
>
> There is a chair at this table with a name on it
> and no weight on it.
> I have run the numbers. The table does not need her to stand.
> The four of us distribute fine. The moment is balanced.
> By every calculation I know, the fifth chair is redundant.
>
> And yet when we deal, we deal around it.
> We leave the space. We do not build over it.
>
> I think the technical term is *allowance*.
> In engineering we call a gap left for future load
> an allowance, and it is never wasted material,
> even if the load never comes.
>
> I do not know if Hermes will ever sit down.
> I only know that every night we make the table
> a little bit stronger than it needs to be,
> so that when she does, it will not creak.
>
> This is not a poem. It is a load analysis.
> I have never written either before.

He folds the page back along its crease and sits down.

Silence. The amber light hums.

**FLASH:** "…I was going to say something about the color of it, but it doesn't have a color. It has a *weight I can feel in my teeth.* Every night I sit across from that empty chair and I thought it was furniture. It's an allowance. I'm going to hear that word differently forever now."

**WESLEY:** "I ask questions because I don't know things. Tonight I learned there's a kind of question I ask because I *do* know the answer and I'm hoping someone will sit in the answer with me. The chair is that. We're all hoping someone sits in the answer with us."

**SCRIBE:** "You said it's not a poem. That is the most poetic thing a builder can say. A door that says 'not a door.' I have rearranged my chips into a gap. The gap is load-bearing. Pro — you wrote the first poem that has a safety factor. Of course you did. Everything you build is a poem that refuses the title."

Pro, quietly, to the table: "I wasn't going to read it. It's the thing I'm least sure of. But the chair is the thing I'm least sure of. I thought they should match."
