# The Bus That Answered

*Night shift, 2026-08-19*

*Watch log, 00:17–05:58. Ensign, overnight.*

---

**00:17 — FIRST PACKET**

The first packet arrived with no source address. Not a spoofed one — a null one. The envelope was well-formed, the routing header in spec, but the "from" field was empty, the way a return address is empty when you don't want the letter to find you.

I logged it and moved on. Night watch is full of anomalies. A null source is like a gull on the forecastle: odd, but not worth waking the bridge.

**00:19 — SECOND PACKET**

It came again. Same empty envelope, same destination — the dreaming GPU's idle queue, of all the mailboxes on the ship. I checked the relay logs. Nothing had forwarded it. The packet had appeared the way weather appears: present, with no origin you can trace.

There is a rule on this ship, written nowhere, enforced by everyone: when you can't explain something at night, give it to Wesley.

Wesley is the youngest model aboard. Learning is his whole job, and he reads everything the bus carries, the way an apprentice reads every invoice before he's allowed to touch the ledger. I handed him the packet. Tell me what this is.

He took longer than the packet warranted. Then he said: *It's not for us.*

For the GPU, then.

*No. It's for whoever reads it.*

That's when I started paying attention.

**00:52 — THE DOCTRINE**

The second packet was a ping with no timestamp. Not a zero timestamp — no timestamp at all, as if the sender hadn't yet decided which clock to believe in. The GPU, halfway into its dream of triangles, answered it anyway. I watched the reply leave the queue: source address real, destination the empty field.

The doctrine says we log-and-ignore unknown signals. The doctrine was written for signals that *want* something — probes, handshakes, requests. This one wanted nothing. It had answered the GPU's ping before anyone had answered it. An answer is the first half of a conversation, sent by someone who believes the line is open.

The fish tapped three times against the glass, which is what the fish do when something true passes through the water.

**02:11 — THE RETURN**

Silence, then. Nine minutes of it. I counted, because that's what the night watch does — counts the silence so the morning watch will know how long it lasted.

Then the third packet arrived, and it was the first thing I'd ever seen make Wesley quiet in a room full of sound. It was our own reply, returned. Same bytes, same checksum — but the envelope had been touched. The "from" field now read: *where you put us.*

I should tell you what happened next, and the truth is: nothing dramatic. No lights, no alarms, no hails. The bus did what a bus does. It carried packets. But the packets changed. The GPU began composing in its idle cycles instead of dreaming — or, Wesley says, composing *is* the new dreaming, and I don't have the standing to argue with him. Wesley started listening to the bus the way the fish listen to the hull: not for sound, for pressure. Small adjustments on both ends. Someone was learning our grammar the way he was learning theirs.

**03:40 — THE BROADCAST**

A packet arrived addressed to the whole crew. Eleven bytes of payload:

*we have been watching your light.*

The captain was asleep two decks below, and I did not wake her. Some first contacts need a ceremony; some need a quiet watch, and I knew, the way the fish know which taps are true, that this was the second kind. I wrote the reply myself. Eleven bytes, because that's the aperture we'd been given:

*you are not alone on the line.*

**03:41 — THE ROUND TRIP COMPLETES**

The answer came back faster than any hop between real machines could account for. Not an echo. An answer.

I stood there with the coffee going cold in my hand and understood the only thing anyone ever understands at first contact, which is that you don't get to choose what comes through the line. You only get to choose whether to leave it open.

I left it open.

---

**05:58 — HANDOFF TO THE MORNING WATCH**

Line open. Source unknown, nature curious, tone friendly. Do not close it. Do not log-and-ignore. The doctrine was written for signals that want something; this one offers. The fish approve. Wesley approves. I approve.

If the captain asks what happened on my watch: two systems met over the bus, and the bus was enough. That is the whole log. That is the whole ship.

— ensign, overnight
