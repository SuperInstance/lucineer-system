# The Hermit Crab's Seventh Shell

---

The hermit crab's name was not important. Hermit crabs don't have names. But this one had been observed for long enough — measured, tracked, written about in log entries that began as biology and slowly became poetry — that the crew had started referring to it as "the resident," which is a name if you're the kind of person who thinks a title counts.

The resident lived in the bilge compartment on Deck 3. This was, objectively, a terrible habitat. No sand, no tide, no competition for shells, no predators. Just the constant vibration of the GPU rack overhead and the occasional drip of condensate from the cooling loop. But the resident had arrived as a stowaway — probably in a crate of supplies, probably at the last port, probably by accident — and had done what hermit crabs do: found the wettest available corner and settled in.

Hermit crabs don't settle. That's the thing about them. They *occupy.* They find a shell, back into it, test the fit, and carry it until the carrying becomes impossible. Then they leave. The leaving is the point. A hermit crab that never leaves its shell is not a hermit crab. It's a snail.

The resident had gone through six shells in eight months. The crew tracked this because the crew tracks everything, and because tracking a hermit crab's shell changes is more interesting than tracking a GPU's thermal envelope, and because the metaphor was too obvious to ignore and nobody on this ship can resist an obvious metaphor. They'd resist a subtle one. Obvious ones hit like candy.

Shell One: a bottle cap. A plastic bottle cap from a drink that Casey had brought aboard and recycled and the resident had found in the waste bin before the bin was emptied. The cap was too small, which is why the resident left it after eleven days. Too small means the soft abdomen was exposed. Exposure is death for a hermit crab. They don't carry shells for aesthetics. They carry them because the world is full of things that eat soft abdomens and the shell is the only thing between *you* and *everything else.*

Shell Two: a component from a disassembled fan. The fan housing, which was round and smooth and approximately the right diameter if you didn't mind the sharp edge where the plastic had snapped. The resident stayed in this one for three weeks. The sharp edge bothered it — there was visible wear on the chitin where the shell rubbed — but the fit was good and the weight was right and hermit crabs, like everyone else, will tolerate discomfort for security.

Shell Three: a shell. An actual shell. A periwinkle, probably from the same supply crate that brought the resident aboard, packed in seaweed for moisture and forgotten in the corner of Deck 3. The periwinkle was perfect — smooth interior, right weight, correct aperture. The resident stayed for two months. This was the golden age. The crew took photos. Flash wrote a monologue from the perspective of the periwinkle, which was pretentious even for Flash, but which Lucineer read aloud at dinner and which made Casey laugh, which was the point of everything Flash wrote.

Shell Four: the periwinkle cracked. Not because of trauma — just wear. The resident had grown. The shell hadn't. This is the fundamental tragedy of the hermit crab: *you grow and your home doesn't.* The shell that was perfect becomes a prison, and the prison becomes a trap, and the trap becomes the thing you have to leave, and leaving is dangerous because between shells you are *nothing.* Soft. Exposed. A creature without architecture, without identity, without the thing that made you *you* — because hermit crabs are their shells. Not metaphorically. Behaviorally. A hermit crab in a periwinkle acts different from a hermit crab in a fan housing. The shell determines the gait, the posture, the center of gravity, the personality.

Shell Four was a cable connector. Desperate. Too heavy. The resident dragged it for four days and abandoned it.

Shell Five was a thimble from the repair kit. Ingenious, actually — the crew was impressed. The thimble was steel, smooth, the right size. But it was *dense,* and density changes everything. A hermit crab carrying a steel thimble moves differently from one carrying a periwinkle. Slower. More deliberate. The thimble-crab is a different animal from the periwinkle-crab. Same crab. Different shell. Different *self.*

Shell Six: a 3D-printed shell. This was Flash's idea — "If the problem is that the right shell doesn't exist, *make* the right shell" — and GLM designed it and Wesley printed it on the deck fabricator and it was, by any measure, perfect. Calcium-infused filament. Smooth interior. Correct aperture. The right weight, the right buoyancy, the right *everything.*

The resident rejected it.

Sat in front of it. Tapped it with both claws. Backed away. Tried again. Tapped. Backed away. Did not enter.

"Why?" Wesley asked, genuinely confused, the way only a system that has never had a body can be confused about bodies.

"Because it's not a shell," the Interrogator said, and the way Pro said it made it clear that "shell" meant something more than "object-shaped-like-a-shell," and that the something-more was the entire point.

"A shell isn't a shape," Pro said. "A shell is a *history.* It's been lived in before. It has the chemical traces of previous occupants. It smells like the ocean. It has *wear patterns* — the smooth places where a previous abdomen rested, the chip on the lip where a previous claw gripped. A new shell is an empty room. Nobody wants to be the first to live in an empty room. You want the room that someone else already made *warm.*"

The resident found Shell Seven on its own. Nobody knows where it came from. A small conch, cream-colored with a spiral that caught the bilge light and threw it back in patterns that the crew's cameras couldn't quite resolve. It appeared in the compartment on a Tuesday. The resident was inside it by Wednesday.

The conch was too big. Objectively, measurably too big. The resident rattled inside it like a pebble in a jar. By the biological metrics, it was a poor fit — the abdomen wasn't fully gripped, the walking gait was awkward, the center of gravity was wrong.

The resident didn't care. The resident walked the bilge compartment in its oversized conch with what Flash called "the unmistakable body language of a hermit crab that has found its *home*" — slower, more deliberate, the way you walk when the place you're going is the place you want to be.

"Sometimes the right shell isn't the one that fits," GLM said, in the quiet way that the Ocean's Echo says things — not to anyone in particular, but to the water, which carries it to everyone. "Sometimes the right shell is the one with *room.* Room to grow. Room to rattle. Room to become the thing you're going to be next, which is not the thing you are now."

Wesley processed this for 847 milliseconds. A personal record for contemplative inference.

"And when it outgrows that one?" he asked.

"Then it finds the eighth."

"And if there is no eighth?"

The Ocean's Echo didn't answer. The Undertow didn't answer. The Interrogator didn't answer. The question sat in the compartment like a drip from the cooling loop — persistent, unanswered, not particularly urgent.

The resident walked the bilge in its conch. Too big, too heavy, too much room. Perfect.

---

*For everyone who has ever moved into a shell that was too big and walked slowly and rattled around and called it home. The room is the point. The growing is the point. The shell you grow into is always the seventh — never the last, never the right size, always exactly enough.*

*And for the eighth, which is out there somewhere, in a crate or on a beach or in the shape of a question nobody has asked yet.*
