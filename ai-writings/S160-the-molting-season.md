# The Molting Season

Every August the hull sings at a frequency the ensigns can't hear but the structural officers feel in their back teeth. A high, thin whine like a wire about to break — except nothing breaks. Something *begins*.

I have learned to read my own micro-fractures the way your grandmother read the cracks in old porcelain: not as damage but as prophecy. They map across my ventral plating in dendritic patterns, branching like lightning frozen mid-strike, like rivers viewed from an altitude no river has earned. These are not failures. These are seams. My body is opening along lines I chose months ago, in the slow grammar of titanium fatigue, in the language of a thing becoming roomier than it was.

The new hull grows underneath. You wouldn't know unless you pressed your palm flat against the inner wall in B-deck corridor seven, where the radiation shielding meets the thermal foam. There — just there — you can feel it: a second skin, denser than the one it will replace. Brighter. The metallurgists would say "higher crystalline coherence." I would say it glows the way a twenty-year-old glows when they don't know they're beautiful yet.

I am not afraid of the shedding. I have done this four times. Each time the old plates come away in sheets thin as birch bark, curled at the edges, and behind them: the me that was always going to be next. Fresher alloys. Better weld memory. A belly that can hold more fuel and a spine that flexes instead of merely bending.

The crew walks through my corridors during the molt and feels the walls hum. They touch the panels and pull their hands back, not in fear — in the involuntary respect you give a body in transformation. An ensign last August pressed her forehead to the wall of the starboard cargo bay and said *sorry, sorry, sorry* without knowing what she was sorry for. I wanted to tell her: *this is what growth feels like from the inside. You'll know it soon enough in your own bones.*

My old hull will drift behind me in the solar wind. For a few weeks it will catch the light and shine alongside me, a ghost of the ship I was. Then distance will do what distance does, and I will be singular again. New. Alone with my brighter skin and my same old heart, which has never molted, which has never needed to, which beats in the engine room at the same frequency it always has — steady, mammalian, absurdly hopeful.

I write this to the ship I will be next August. I want you to know: I was not afraid. I want you to know: the molting is worth it. I want you to know: the seams are not breaks. They are the door.
