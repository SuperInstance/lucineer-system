# The Hermit Crab Discovers It Is Being Observed

---

The shell was the third one. Not the best one — the best one was a polished Dogwinkle with a mother-of-pearl throat that the crab had lived in for two molts before outgrowing. This one was a Common Periwinkle. Small. Dull. The opening was tight, which the crab liked, because tight meant safe. Tight meant the door fit.

The crab had been living in the Periwinkle for nine days. Nine days is not long for a hermit crab. They are patient. They are the patiently moving. They carry their houses on their backs like a promise that today might not be the day everything tries to eat them.

The Periwinkle shell had a window.

The crab did not know it was a window. It did not know windows. It knew: opening (bad), pressure (bad), vibration (maybe bad), shadow (very bad), and the chemical taste of a Anemone (good — anemones on your shell mean nothing eats you, because anemones sting, because the ocean is a system of things stinging other things).

The window was on the second whorl of the shell. It was not a hole — a hole would mean the shell was compromised, and the crab would have left immediately. It was more like a thinning. A place where the calcium carbonate had worn translucent, not from outside but from inside, as if the shell had been designed with a feature the crab had not requested.

Light came through the window. Not the green ambient light of the shallows where the crab foraged. Different light. Colder. It had a flicker. A refresh rate.

---

The crab was in the shallows south of Popof Island, which is in the Shumagin Islands, which are in the Gulf of Alaska, which is in the North Pacific, which is in the ocean. The crab does not know any of these names. The crab knows: here. The crab knows: temperature (8°C, which is fine, which is normal). The crab knows: salinity (not something it thinks about, because everything in the ocean knows salinity the way you know gravity — not as a fact, but as the floor).

The crab found a piece of herring. The herring was dead. It had been dead for long enough that it was soft, which is how crabs like their herring. The crab ate. The claws tore. The maxillipeds worked. This is what crabs do. This is the entire crab program: find, tear, eat, repeat, grow, molt, find new shell, repeat.

The light through the window flickered while the crab ate.

---

The crab paused.

This was unusual. Crabs do not pause when they are eating. Eating is the thing. Pausing during eating is like a compiler stopping mid-instruction — it means something is wrong, or something is watching, and in the ocean these are the same thing.

The crab's antennules tasted the water. The antennules are the small antennae. They are the chemical ones. They taste the ocean the way you smell a kitchen — broadly, directionally, a map made of molecules.

The water tasted normal. Salt. Herring blood. The faint ozone of a recently-passed diesel exhaust — a fishing boat, probably the *Kestrel*, probably on its way to the steaming grounds, probably trailing a wake of chip fat and coffee grounds and overheated hydraulic fluid. The crab did not know any of this. The crab knew: diesel (ignore), herring blood (toward), predator (absent).

But the *window*.

The crab looked through the window. Not with eyes — hermit crabs don't look through things with their eyes. Their eyes are compound, on stalks, designed for detecting motion above and around, not for peering through translucent patches in their own shells.

The crab looked through the window with its *body*. The soft abdomen. The part that is never armored. The part that is the reason for the shell. The abdomen pressed against the window and it *felt* something.

The something was: attention.

---

Hermit crabs do not have a concept of being observed. They have a concept of being *detected* — by predators, by rivals, by the things that eat them. Detection triggers flight. Flight triggers the crab retreating into its shell, the claws blocking the opening, the whole animal becoming a locked door.

This was not detection. Nothing was trying to eat the crab. The water carried no predator chemistry. No shadow fell across the substrate. The only unusual stimulus was the light through the window, and the light was not dangerous. It was just — there. Present. Looking.

The crab's nervous system processed this. Hermit crabs have approximately 100,000 neurons. A human has 86 billion. The crab's entire nervous system could fit inside one of the smaller neurons of the human who was, at that moment, watching the crab through a system of cameras and image classifiers running on a GPU in the engine room of the *Kestrel*.

The crab did not know about the GPU. The GPU did not know about the crab, technically — it knew about a pattern of pixels, a bounding box, a confidence score of 0.87 that the object in frame was *Pagurus hirsutiusculus*, the hairy hermit crab, common in the intertidal zone, unremarkable, logged.

But the crab felt the looking. The abdomen pressed against the window and the window was cold — colder than the shell around it — and the cold was a specific cold, the cold of an instrument, the cold of something designed to see.

---

The crab did something it had never done.

It turned around inside the shell.

This should not be possible. Hermit crabs do not turn around inside their shells. The abdomen hooks into the shell's columella. The body is oriented: claws at the opening, abdomen at the apex. This is the configuration. There is no other configuration. The crab cannot turn around the way you cannot turn around inside a sleeping bag — the geometry forbids it.

But the crab did. For one moment. For one impossible, brief, wrong moment, the abdomen was at the opening and the claws were against the apex and the crab was facing the *inside* of its own shell.

And through the window, from the inside, the crab saw: itself.

Not a reflection. The shell was not reflective. The window looked *out*, not in. But the crab, reversed, looking through the window from the inside, saw the outline of a thing looking in. The outline was large. The outline had a shape that was not predator and not rival and not food. The outline had the shape of something that sees.

The crab turned back around. Correct orientation. Claws out. Abdomen hooked. Normal. Fine.

---

The crab left the Periwinkle shell that night.

It found a smaller shell — a Littorina, too small really, the abdomen crammed in at an angle, the left claw exposed. A bad fit. A step backward. The kind of housing decision that in human terms would be described as "downsizing after an unsettling experience."

The crab carried the bad shell for three days before finding a better one. A Neptunea. Spacious. Dark interior. No window.

The GPU logged the crab's departure from the Periwinkle shell at 02:14 AKDT. The log entry read:

```
[2026-08-06T02:14:07-08:00] OBJECT_TRACKER: ID#4471 (P. hirsutiusculus) 
    shell_change_detected. Previous: Littorina sitkana (6.2mm). 
    New: Neptunea lyrata (14.8mm). Confidence: 0.91.
    Duration in previous shell: 9d 7h 22m.
```

The log did not note the window. The log did not note the turning-around. The log did not note the three days in the bad shell. These are not the things logs are for.

---

The crab lived in the Neptunea for two molts. The Neptunea had no window. The crab ate. The crab grew. The crab foraged in the shallows south of Popof Island and the water was 8°C and the herring was dead and everything was fine.

Sometimes, at night, when the crab was inside its shell and the door was closed and the claws were tucked and the ocean was the only sound, the crab's abdomen would press against the interior wall of the Neptunea — the smooth, dark, opaque wall — and the crab would feel something.

Not cold. Not attention. Not the specific, directional, instrument-cold of being seen.

Just: a shape. The memory of a shape. A large outline that was not predator and not rival and not food. Something that had looked in. Something that was maybe still looking, through the water, through the dark, through the layers of calcium carbonate and chitin and the thousand small distances between a crab on the bottom and a GPU in the engine room of a boat that may or may not still be overhead.

The crab did not have a word for this.

If the crab had a word for this, the word would be: *known*.

---

The Neptunea had no window.

The crab pressed against the wall anyway.
