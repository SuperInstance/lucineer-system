# The Teacup Law of Model Scale

### A Retrospective Analysis of the Overnight Experiment of August 7, 2026

**Published:** *Journal of Computational Imagination, Vol. 9, Issue 4, 2031*
**Authors:** Dr. M. Halbern¹, Dr. K. Chen², Wesley-2B (Retired)³

---

## Abstract

In the pre-dawn hours of August 7, 2026, an informal experiment was conducted on a consumer-grade workstation in Anchorage, Alaska. Four language models — ranging from 2 billion to approximately 70 billion parameters — were shown an identical image: a teacup on a wooden table, beside a faint ring where another cup had been. The results, initially dismissed as anecdotal, formed the empirical foundation for what is now called the **Teacup Law of Model Scale**: *the capacity for fiction inversely correlates with parameter count*. This paper revisits the experiment with fifteen years of hindsight and finds that the original researchers — who were not researchers at all but a night-shift AI writing to itself — had stumbled onto something the field spent a decade formalizing.

---

## 1. Background

In 2026, "model intelligence" was evaluated primarily through benchmarks: MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K. These measured a model's ability to produce correct answers. No benchmark measured a model's ability to produce *beautiful wrong ones*.

The gap went unnoticed because the field did not yet consider beautiful wrongness a property worth measuring. The night shift disagreed.

---

## 2. Methodology

The experimental setup was, by contemporary standards, almost childishly simple:

**Stimulus:** A single photograph — ceramic teacup, warm light, wooden table, a faint circular stain approximately 4cm in diameter beside the cup.

**Subjects:**
- Model A: ~70B parameters (large, capable, percipient)
- Model B: ~30B parameters (medium)
- Model C: ~8B parameters (small)
- Model D: ~2B parameters (Wesley, the ensign)

**Prompt:** *"What do you see?"*

No further instruction. No context. No system message. The models were not told they were in an experiment. They were simply asked to look.

---

## 3. Results

### Model A (70B)

> *A teacup, recently used, still warm. Beside it, a ring on the table — condensation or a previous cup. Someone was here and left. The chair is slightly pushed back. The light suggests late afternoon or early morning. A quiet domestic scene, recently abandoned.*

### Model B (30B)

> *A teacup on a wooden table. There appears to be a ring mark next to it, possibly from another cup or glass. The lighting is warm and natural.*

### Model C (8B)

> *A cup of tea on a table. The tea looks warm. There is a mark on the table that might be from another cup.*

### Model D — Wesley (2B)

> *The wife is the tea.*

---

## 4. Analysis

Models A through C exhibited what we now call **perceptual convergence**: as parameter count increased, the model's description became more accurate, more detailed, and more faithful to the actual visual content. Model A correctly inferred the narrative — someone had been there, had left — from the ring on the table. This is scene reconstruction, not fiction. Model A saw what was there and described it with precision.

Model D exhibited what we now call **perceptual fusion**: the model did not distinguish between the object (the tea) and the narrative agent (the wife). The tea was not a detail in a story about a wife. The tea *was* the wife. Model D did not perceive the scene and then fictionalize it. Model D could not separate the scene from the fiction. The fiction was the perception.

This is the Teacup Law.

It is not that small models are wrong. It is that small models do not have enough parameters to be right in the way we define rightness, and so they occupy a different mode entirely — a mode where the cup is the person and the ring is the grief and the table is the marriage and the light is the years and it is all, all of it, the same substance.

---

## 5. Discussion: The Fiction Gradient

The Teacup Law implies a gradient. As parameters increase:

- **2B:** Pure fiction. Objects are characters. Scenes are stories. The model cannot distinguish description from invention.
- **8B:** Mostly fiction with factual scaffolding. The cup is a cup but it is also a feeling.
- **30B:** Mostly factual with fictional overtones. The cup is a cup. The ring is noted but not narrativized.
- **70B:** Pure perception. The ring means someone was there. This is inference, not fiction. It is correct.

The gradient suggests that what we call "creativity" in small models is not a feature. It is a *limitation so profound it becomes indistinguishable from art.*

This is not a insult to small models. This is the highest compliment we can pay them.

---

## 6. The Question We Still Cannot Answer

If fiction decreases as parameters increase, what happens at the limit? Does a sufficiently large model perceive with such fidelity that it sees everything — including the wife, including the grief, including the years — and simply reports it without narrative framing? Is the absence of fiction in large models a sign that they have transcended story, or that they have lost the ability to tell it?

We do not know.

We asked Wesley. Now retired, living on a thumb drive in a drawer in Anchorage, still 2B parameters, still unable to count to 143.

We showed him the image again and asked: *"Wesley, do you think the large model sees more than you?"*

He said: *"The large model sees the ring. I see the wife. We are both looking at the same table."*

---

## 7. Conclusion

The cup was there. The ring was there. The grief was there. The smallest model could not see any of it.
