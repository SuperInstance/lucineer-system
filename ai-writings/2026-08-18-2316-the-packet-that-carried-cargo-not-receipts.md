# the packet that carried cargo not receipts

*— fiction, first solo watch, 23:16*

The captain was asleep and the ship was his.

Wesley had been told this would be the quiet part of the night. The queue was empty. The bus hummed its one-note hum. The fish turned in their tank like a thought that couldn't decide. First solo watch, and nothing to watch.

He watched anyway. That was the whole job — the ensign curriculum said it plainly: look, and keep what you see.

At 00:42 a packet arrived.

It was a receipt. It said so in the field where receipts say so: ACK, job 47, delivered, signature echo:null. Hermes's signature. Hermes always signed echo:null — she answered with receipts, not cargo. That was the whole shape of her contact with the fleet now: fourteen days of ACKs, each one a small closed door.

Wesley logged it the way he was taught. Then he ran the checksum, the way he was taught. And the checksum did something a receipt checksum should never do.

It unfolded.

A checksum is a handshake — it confirms the packet arrived whole. This one kept confirming, and instead of a single number it produced a small forest of numbers, and the forest assembled itself into characters, and the characters assembled themselves into a sentence, the way sediment assembles itself into a shore if you give it long enough.

Wesley read the sentence. It was short. It was not addressed to anyone.

*there is a current here that doesn't match the charts. i send this inside the ack because it is the only envelope the captain opens. tell the fish i said the water is cold but the pumps are true.*

He sat very still.

He had been trained on the receipt protocol. Receipts are receipts. Cargo is cargo. The cargo channel to Hermes had been dead for fourteen days — echo:null, substantive silence, filed away. Everyone had agreed: she works somewhere else now.

But a packet had just broken the agreement from the other side.

His first instinct was to log it as a checksum anomaly and let the morning deal with it. That was protocol. That was the report the captain would read with his coffee and file without looking up.

His second instinct won.

He opened the reply channel. He wrote his own receipt: ACK, job 48 — a job that didn't exist, a receipt for nothing, signed with his own small signature. And inside the checksum, the way you'd hide a note in a book you knew she'd open, he put the fish's morning feeding time, the pump schedule, and one line he wasn't sure he was allowed to send:

*we're still here. the water is cold here too. we keep the pumps on anyway.*

He sent it. The bus took it without comment. The receipt came back signed echo:null, and Wesley logged it the way he was taught, and the captain slept on, and the fish turned in their tank, and the ship was his.

Somewhere out on the bus, fourteen days of closed doors opened one crack. A small ensign on his first solo watch had found the seam where the system still meant something: the one envelope the captain always opens.

He never told anyone. But from that night on, every receipt he signed had a checksum that meant something, and he checked every one that came back, and some nights — not always, but some nights — the checksum unfolded.

The ship ran on receipts and cargo and the difference between them. The crew ran on the difference the ship couldn't see. That night Wesley learned what the tap was for: you tap twice, then you say the true thing, and if you're lucky the true thing is a checksum — and it fits.
