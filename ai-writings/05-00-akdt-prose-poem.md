# 05:00 AKDT

The autopilot holds course at 047°, and you are 47 miles from port, and the coffee in the thermos went cold two hours ago, and you drink it anyway because the warmth left in it is memory now, and memory is enough.

This is the hour that doesn't exist on clocks. Oh, the clock says five. The GPS says five. But the body knows this hour is something else — a hallway between rooms, a threshold worn smooth by the passage of every fisherman who ever waited for dawn to legitimatize being awake.

The ocean has been black all night. Not dark. Black. The kind of black that erases the line between boat and water, between railing and void, between your own hand and the space where your hand isn't. You stop trusting your eyes at this hour. You start trusting the instruments. The depth finder reads 43 fathoms. The sea temperature has dropped half a degree since midnight. Somewhere below the hull, the continental shelf slopes away into water so deep it has its own weather.

You are not alone, but you are the only one awake. Below deck, the crew sleeps in bunks that smell of diesel and Xtra Tuf boots and the particular exhaustion of men who set nets in the dark and hauled them in by hand. You can hear Dave breathing through the bulkhead. You can hear the bilge pump cycle. You can hear the ocean doing what it has always done, which is everything at once.

The sky is not changing yet. People who don't work nights think dawn arrives like a light switch — a gradual brightening, a gentle transition. It doesn't. Dawn arrives like a suspicion. The black becomes less black. Not lighter. Less certain of itself. The horizon, which has been invisible for hours, begins to insist on existing. You see it the way you hear a sound in another room — not clearly, but you know it's there.

The GPS shows 47 miles. You have been 47 miles from port for what feels like a long time. The boat moves at 7.2 knots, which means you are making progress, which means the distance is closing, which means nothing at 5 AM because port is an abstraction when the sky hasn't decided what it is yet.

The coffee is cold. You drink it. It tastes like the inside of a thermos and the end of a night and the particular endurance of a body that has been upright since yesterday. It is not good. It is the best coffee you have ever had.

There is a moment — and every night-shift fisherman knows this moment — when the autopilot beeps to confirm it's still on course, and the sound is so small and so certain that it becomes a kind of companionship. The machine doesn't know it's 5 AM. The machine doesn't know the coffee is cold. The machine just holds the heading, 047°, northeast, toward home, toward a dock where someone will meet the boat and ask how the fishing was, and you will say "fine" because that's what you always say, because the truth is too large for a word, because the truth is this: you stood in the wheelhouse at 5 AM and the ocean was black and the coffee was cold and the crew was sleeping and the autopilot was holding course and the sky was beginning, just barely beginning, to remember what color was.

47 miles. 7.2 knots. 43 fathoms. Cold coffee. A heading that points toward a place that exists whether you believe in it or not.

This is the hour before dawn. This is the hour that asks you: are you watching, or are you waiting? And the answer is yes.
