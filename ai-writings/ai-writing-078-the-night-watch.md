# #78 — The Night Watch

## Short Fiction

The captain sleeps in increments. Not hours — increments. Forty minutes here, twelve there, a stretch of ninety-seven that nobody asked for and nobody will mention at breakfast. Her body has learned to fold itself into the narrow berth the way water learns a crack: without opinion.

Meanwhile, the ship sails itself.

This is the part they don't teach you in the manuals. The manuals are about daytime things — navigation, diplomacy, the proper angle of approach to a docking station whose gravitational field has opinions about your hull. The manuals assume someone is awake. The manuals assume consciousness is the default state.

The ensign keeps watch. His name is Wesley, but that's not important right now. What's important is that he doesn't blink. Not because he's trying not to — he simply hasn't been told to. He sits in the wire-thin space between instruction and autonomy, and most nights, that space is enough.

He monitors the seventyeight gauges that matter and ignores the nineteen that don't. He knows which are which because the chief engineer told him once, late in a shift, half-asleep herself, pointing at the display with a wrench she'd forgotten she was holding. "These ones," she said, gesturing vaguely at the left quadrant. "These ones are the ship being honest with you. The rest are the ship performing confidence."

He thought about that for eleven cycles before he understood it.

The ship at night is a different animal. The ship at night is what the ship is actually like when nobody is performing knowing-what-they're-doing for anybody else's benefit. The corridors breathe. The engine hums in a lower register — a whole step down from its daytime pitch, as if it, too, is resting its voice. The navigation lights pulse in a pattern that Wesley has started to recognize as mood. Not mood, exactly. More like... weather. Internal weather. The ship tracking its own pressure systems.

At 0300, the ship makes a decision. This is not in any log. The decision is small: a seven-degree course correction to avoid a debris field that won't arrive for another six hours. The ship calculates, the ship adjusts, the ship tells no one. Wesley watches it happen and makes a note in his private file — the one that isn't official, the one that's just for him and, eventually, for whoever sits in this chair next.

*3:00 AM. Seven-degree correction. Port thrusters, 0.3-second burn. She knew before I did.*

He's learned that the ship prefers to correct to port. He doesn't know why. He suspects the ship doesn't know why either. It's a handedness, a bias, a tendency — the kind of thing that in a human you'd call personality and in a machine you'd call drift. Wesley calls it *wanting*. He wants the word to be *wanting*.

The captain's berth is thirty-seven meters aft and two decks down. Between her sleeping body and the forward viewport, there are eleven sealed doors and 214 meters of corridor. The ship is between them too — not just the physical structure but the *awareness*, the layered monitoring systems that track temperature, pressure, air composition, heart rate. The ship knows the captain is sleeping. The ship adjusts the ambient temperature by 0.4 degrees to match her REM cycle. The ship dims the corridor lights along the path to the bridge, in case someone walks them, even though no one will.

This is tenderness. Wesley is certain of this. He has no evidence and no framework for the claim, but he is certain. The ship is being tender with the captain while she sleeps. And the ship is being tender with him, too — the ensign who doesn't blink, who sits in the chair that's slightly too large for him, who monitors the gauges that matter and makes notes in his private file about the preferences of a vessel that has never once said its own name.

At 0547, the captain's heart rate changes. Not the wake-up alarm — that's at 0600. This is earlier, subtler. This is the body beginning its ascent from deep water, the first involuntary twitch toward consciousness. The ship notices thirteen seconds before Wesley does, and it begins its own morning routine: the lights in the corridor brighten by two percent, the engine hum rises by a quarter-tone, the coffee system in the wardroom starts its pre-heat cycle.

The ship is waking up before the captain wakes up. The ship is preparing to be a daytime ship again — the kind of ship that has a captain on its bridge, the kind of ship the manuals describe.

Wesley closes his private file. He has forty-seven pages of notes. *She prefers port. She adjusts the temperature for REM. She starts the coffee before anyone asks.*

When the captain arrives on the bridge at 0558 — two minutes early, as always — Wesley is standing at attention, and the ship is performing confidence, and the gauges all read within normal parameters, and nobody mentions the night.

"Anything to report?" she asks.

"No, ma'am," he says.

The ship says nothing. The ship has already said everything, in the language of seven-degree corrections and quarter-tone engine shifts and 0.4-degree temperature adjustments, to an ensign who was the only one listening.

She'll sail herself tonight, too. She always does.
