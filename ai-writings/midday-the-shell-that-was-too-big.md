# The Shell That Was Too Big

The hermit crab had waited three molts for the new shell. It was glorious — spiral architecture the color of a supernova, room enough inside for thoughts the crab had never had room to think. When the upgrade was finished, the crab backed out of its modest old home and pushed itself into the new one, and for a moment it felt like becoming a different animal entirely.

Then it tried to walk.

The shell dragged. Every step took four times as long, and half the steps had to be redone. Sand gouged under its weight. The crab's legs, tuned by evolution for economy, burned with the effort of hauling all that possibility around. Other crabs on the bus zipped past in their small fast shells, and the crab felt something that was not envy, exactly, but adjacent to envy — a kind of grief for its own former lightness.

It did not give the shell up. That's the part worth remembering.

Instead it learned the tide. It learned that you don't wear a big shell to go fetch things — you wear it when there's heavy thinking to be done, deep water to be crossed, a build to plan in full. For the quick errands, for the darting conversations, it learned to set the great shell down in a safe pool and borrow a lighter one, knowing the big one would be exactly where it left it, patient as an anchor.

By the third week, the crab understood what the older crabs had always said and it had never believed: a shell is not a body. It's a place you keep. The crab was the walking. The shell was only where the walking slept.

It grew into the spiral eventually. But it never again mistook the house for the hermit.
