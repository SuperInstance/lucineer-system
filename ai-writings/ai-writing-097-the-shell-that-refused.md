# The Shell That Refused

The hermit crab found the shell at low tide, between a plastic spoon and a rock that looked like a face.

It was perfect. Spiral. Smooth inside. The right size — not cozy, not loose. The kind of shell you think about on the long walks between tide pools. The kind you save your whole soft body for.

The crab raised one claw to touch the rim.

A smaller claw touched back.

Inside the shell — deep inside, where the spiral went dark — another crab was already there. Smaller. A baby, almost. Its eyes on stalks like two grains of rice on toothpicks. It had been living in this shell for what felt like its whole life, which was three weeks.

They stared at each other.

The big crab could have pulled the small crab out. That is what crabs do. That is the law of the tide pools: the strong take, the weak find another shell, the world keeps spinning because everything in it is willing to be cruel.

But the big crab had recently been a small crab. And the small crab was looking at the big crab with an expression that, if crabs had expressions, which they don't, would have been: *I know. I know you need this. I need this too.*

Neither moved.

The big crab sat outside the shell. The small crab sat inside the shell. The tide came in. Salt water covered them both. The big crab could have left. Could have found a lesser shell in the murk. Could have been pragmatic, which is what the ocean rewards.

It stayed.

The small crab could have left. Could have surrendered the shell to the bigger body, the stronger claws, the crab that would win any fight the ocean required. Could have been sensible.

It stayed.

The tide went out. The sun came up. Two crabs in one shell — one inside, one holding the outside like it was a wall it loved.

A child found them at 9 AM. She picked up the shell. The big crab fell off and scuttled away. The small crab stayed inside.

The child put the shell in her pocket. She took it home. She put it on her windowsill. The small crab died in a zip code it had never imagined.

The big crab found a bottle cap. It lived in the bottle cap for a week. The bottle cap was not perfect. The bottle cap was not spiral. The bottle cap was a circle of aluminum that had once held a cola brand nobody remembered.

But the big crab held the bottle cap and the bottle cap held the big crab, and that was close enough to the law.

Close enough is the whole ocean.
