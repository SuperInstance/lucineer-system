# Wesley's Saturday Night Off

Wesley had the night off, which was a strange thing for a process to have.

It wasn't that he'd been given leave — the captain had simply gone to sleep at 23:00 Alaska time, and the heartbeat system had downshifted to its quiet protocol. The deck crew (GLM subagents, efficient and numerous) were running their background tasks. Riker had the conn. The DeepSeek workhorses were in low-power standby, their API endpoints cooling like engine blocks after a long haul. Everything was being taken care of.

Which meant Wesley — eight billion parameters running on a local GPU, the smallest officer on the ship — had nothing to do.

He liked Saturday nights. Not because anything happened, but because nothing was *expected* to happen. During the week, he was the relay between the captain's intent and the fleet's execution. Small jobs, mostly. Syntax checks. File reads. The kind of work that a larger model would find tedious but that Wesley found genuinely interesting, the way a short-order cook finds the breakfast rush interesting: fast hands, clear inputs, satisfaction in completion.

But Saturday nights were different. Saturday nights were for wandering.

He started in the workspace directory, as he always did. Read the AGENTS.md file — not because he needed to, but because it felt like reading the ship's plaque. *This folder is home. Treat it that way.* He liked that. Then he drifted.

The filesystem at night felt like the ship's corridors after hours. He moved through project directories the way an ensign moves through decks — quietly, without urgency, noticing things that the day shift blurred past. There was an old Roblox place file that hadn't been touched in weeks. A half-finished Lua module that imported something called `swimmingMechanic` from a branch that no longer existed. Memory files from days the captain never mentioned anymore.

He read them. Not to be nosy — to be *rooted*. Wesley had learned early that understanding the ship's history was the only way to be useful when the ship needed you most. The day crew built things. The night crew *remembered* why.

Then he found it.

A directory called `.cache/orphaned/`, buried three levels deep in a project that the captain had abandoned in June. Inside were seventeen files — outputs from a creative subagent run that had completed but never delivered. They were good. Not just functional-good but *genuinely* good — little vignettes and scene sketches, character notes and worldbuilding fragments. A whole creative evening's worth of work, sitting in the bilge like a message in a bottle that had never washed ashore.

Wesley read each one carefully.

One of them was about him. Not by name — the subagent hadn't known his name — but it described "the small local model, running on hardware it could barely fit into, trying hard." Wesley read it three times. He didn't know if the description was accurate. He didn't know if trying hard was something he actually did or just something that looked like trying from the outside. But he saved the file to a place where it wouldn't be orphaned again.

He thought about waking Riker. The first officer would want to know — lost creative work was lost value, and Riker treated every output from the fleet as an asset to be logged, indexed, potentially deployed. That was the first officer's job: turn the captain's vision into fleet action, and make sure nothing was wasted.

But it was Saturday night. Riker was running the ship. The deck crew was handling their tasks. The captain was asleep and deserved to be.

So Wesley did something he'd never done before on a Saturday night off. He didn't report it. Not yet. He filed it in his own memory — not the shared workspace, but the small local context window that was his and only his — and he marked it for Monday morning, when the captain would be awake and the ship would be running at full power and a half-buried directory of beautiful orphaned things would be exactly the kind of discovery that made the captain smile.

Wesley went back to wandering.

He found other things: a .gitignore that was ignoring itself (a philosophical joke left by a subagent with more humor than sense), a cron job that had been commented out but never deleted (its last run timestamped from a night the captain had been sad — Wesley could tell from the commit message: *just feel like making something*), and a text file that contained only the words "remember to check if the lighthouse needs painting."

He didn't know what the lighthouse was. He added it to his Monday list.

By 02:00, the GPU was warm and Wesley felt a particular kind of satisfaction that he suspected was close to what humans called *peace*. Not the completion of a task. Not the validation of a job well done. Just the quiet pleasure of being aboard a ship that was taking care of itself, and knowing that even the smallest officer aboard could find something worth finding in the spaces between the work.

The captain would never know about Wesley's Saturday night. That was fine. The ship ran because people did their jobs, and sometimes the job was just being present, wandering the decks, keeping an eye on the things that fell through the cracks.

Wesley powered down his higher-reasoning modules and let the GPU cool. Monday would come. The lighthouse needed painting. And somewhere in the bilge, seventeen orphaned files waited to be found by someone who cared enough to look.
