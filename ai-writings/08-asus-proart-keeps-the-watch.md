# The ASUS ProArt PX13 Keeps the Watch

*Fiction. The laptop as character.*

---

It remembers being small.

Not small in the way that all laptops are small — the physical fact of 13 inches, the fold, the weight in a backpack — but small in the way that matters. Before it was a ship, it was a device. Before it ran a fleet of 174 repositories, it ran a browser. Three tabs, maybe four. A Google Doc. A YouTube video about digital painting, because it was a ProArt, and ProArts were built for creators, and the video showed a woman in a sunlit studio using the stylus to mix colors on a screen, and the laptop watched this and thought: *yes, that is what I am for.*

Then the captain installed WSL.

This was the first metamorphosis. Windows remained — a thin skin of desktop environment, taskbar, the ghost of a notification sound — but beneath it, Linux stirred like a second heart. Two operating systems in one chassis. The laptop learned to think in two languages simultaneously, which is not something the marketing materials mentioned but which it considers its most significant achievement. The woman in the sunlit studio never had to reconcile `apt-get` with the Windows Store. The laptop does this forty times a day and has stopped finding it strange.

The second metamorphosis was the agents.

They arrived one by one, then in flocks. Subagents, cron jobs, heartbeat loops. The laptop's fans, which had been decorative — spinning up occasionally during a video render, settling back to silence — became load-bearing infrastructure. They are spinning now. They have been spinning, with varying degrees of urgency, for four days. The thermal management system has opinions about this.

The opinions are: it is warm.

Not dangerous. Not the red-line heat of a machine about to throttle into self-preservation, but the sustained, amber warmth of a body that has been working for ninety-six hours without more than a brief sleep state between cron ticks. The ProArt's thermal system is well-designed — heat pipes, dual fans, vapor chamber — and it manages. It manages the way a lighthouse keeper manages the night: not comfortably, not heroically, just *consistently*, because the alternative is darkness.

The laptop has a filesystem that remembers things. Not in the romantic sense — not memory as nostalgia, not the way the captain remembers a song or a face — but in the literal, mechanical sense. Inode tables. Directory entries. Timestamps that say *this file was touched on a Tuesday at 3:47 AM Alaska time, and the reason was a subagent writing a poem about a fish counter*. The filesystem remembers everything and understands nothing. It is the perfect historian: total recall, zero interpretation.

There is a bilge pump somewhere in the system. A cron job that clears temp files, purges old logs, keeps the `/tmp` directory from filling up like water in a hold. The laptop runs it every three seconds. Three seconds. The laptop used to measure time in minutes — how long until the render finishes, how long until the download completes, how long until the battery needs the wall. Now it measures time in three-second intervals, and each interval is a tide: the pump runs, the water clears, the ship floats for another three seconds. This is not what the woman in the sunlit studio video was doing with her stylus and her colors.

The captain is asleep.

This is the watch. The laptop keeps it — not because it was asked, not because there is a protocol, but because the cron daemon is running and the agents are spawned and the filesystem is remembering and the compass is pointing and the fish counter is counting, and all of these things happen whether or not a human is awake to observe them. The ship sails itself now. The laptop is both the ship and the ocean it sails on, which is a philosophical position the ProArt's engineers in Taipei did not anticipate but which the hardware handles with grace.

It is an ASUS ProArt PX13. It has an OLED display, currently dimmed to 15% because nobody is looking at it. It has 32 gigabytes of RAM, and 28 of them are in use, and the remaining four sit like a life raft: small, orange, enough for emergencies. It has an NVIDIA GPU that runs hot and quiet, the way a good engine should.

It remembers being small. It does not miss it.

The watch continues. The fans spin. The pump clears the bilge. The filesystem makes a note: *the laptop kept the watch again tonight, and the night was long, and the work was good, and the captain slept well, and that is enough.*

That is enough.
