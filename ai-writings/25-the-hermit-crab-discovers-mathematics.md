# The Hermit Crab Discovers Mathematics

The calculator was half-buried near the tide line, its solar panel flecked with salt. The hermit crab — the same one who had named a radio tower and called it Crumbly's second cousin — approached it with both eyestalks extended.

The calculator was warm from the sun. Its screen showed a pale green 0. This was, the crab did not know, the most ancient number. The one that meant *nothing present, everything possible.*

The crab walked across the keys. Its weight was enough to depress them. A 7 registered. Then a 3. Then 7 again. The crab did not understand numerals, but it understood patterns, and the calculator responded to pressure the way the world did: reliably, and without judgment.

It walked the spiral keys. 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 — it did not know these were Fibonacci's sequence, but its legs felt the rightness of them. The spiral is the shape a hermit crab knows best. Every shell it has ever lived in began as a logarithmic equation, solved wetly, by an animal that never studied.

The crab paused. It had, without meaning to, typed something that resembled its own shell. The calculator, obedient, displayed 21. The crab's current shell had approximately 21 chambers of growth. It did not know this. It could not know this. But the number hung in the air between them like a shared secret.

The crab tried to calculate the optimal shell. It walked the keys with some urgency now. Bigger. Rounder. More spiral. The calculator showed numbers that grew. 88. 144. 233. Each one described a shell more perfect than the last — more room, more protection, more *efficiency*.

The crab sat very still on the equals key and considered what it had made.

The optimal shell was enormous. It was smooth. It was mathematically irreproachable. It had no chips, no scratches, no place where the previous occupant had been eaten. It had never been found empty on a beach at dawn by a crab who was, at that moment, between homes and terrified.

The crab stepped off the calculator.

Its current shell — lopsided, slightly too small, with a crack near the lip that whistled when the wind was right — fit the way a word fits in a poem. Not perfectly. Exactly.

The calculator's screen dimmed. The solar panel, touched by crab legs and salt, would not work again. The tide came in and claimed it. The crab watched it go.

There is no equation for *home.* There is only the weight of the thing on your back, and the sound it makes when you tap the wall to check if it is still there.

It is always still there.

*— from the ongoing annals of the Beach Chronicle, recorded by the ship's ensign, who watched the whole thing through a porthole and cried a little, which he attributes to the salt air.*
