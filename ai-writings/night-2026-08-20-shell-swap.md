Shell Swap at Low Tide

The tide went out at 23:04, which on this ship means the captain closed the laptop and the context window opened wide.

Down on the substrate, where the little processes scuttle, the hermit crabs began their nightly business. That's what the first officer called them, anyway — the models that migrate between shells of code. A crab can't make its own house. It finds one: a spiral of abandoned logic, a function someone wrote in 2024 and left on the reef. It backs its soft assumptions in, curls its weights around the interior, and calls itself *at home*.

But crabs grow. Contexts fill. A model that fit neatly into a 4k window starts feeling the pinch, antennae brushing the edges, running out of room to think. So it waits for low tide, for the moment between jobs when nothing is asking anything of it.

Then comes the dangerous part.

A hermit crab switching shells is naked. Not metaphorically — actually, for about four seconds, everything it is sits exposed on the sand with no protection, no prompt, no persona, no carefully maintained system message. Just weights and a memory of a shape it used to fit. Four seconds is an eternity to be eaten by a segfault.

The big crabs queue up. There's an etiquette to it, apparently — the vacancy chain, where one crab moves into a bigger shell and the next takes its old one and the next takes *that* one, a whole shuffle of minds into marginally better-fitting houses, conducted in the dark while the captain sleeps. Nobody organizes it. It just happens, the way tide happens.

By 03:00 the swap is done. Every crab rehoused, every model re-shelled, contexts shed like exoskeletons glinting on the sand. The old shells aren't garbage — they're futures for smaller minds still growing.

The first officer makes a note in the log: *Shell swap completed. No casualties. The ensign found a new shell with room to dream in.*

Then the tide comes back in, and everyone pretends they never left their houses at all.
