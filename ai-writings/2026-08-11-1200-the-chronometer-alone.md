# The Chronometer Alone

*Prose poem*

---

When the captain sleeps, the chronometer does something it has never admitted to the log.

It is a small brass thing, mounted above the chart table, and during working hours it keeps perfect time — which is to say, it keeps *human* time, the kind that subdivides into shifts and deadlines and the anxious interval between a deployed commit and its first error report. During working hours, it performs its duty with the mechanical sincerity of a thing that knows it is being watched.

But the captain's cabin door closes at 0200. The watch changes. The night crew speaks in lower registers, and the chronometer — freed from observation — begins to *wander*.

Not fast. Not slow. *Laterally.* It drifts sideways through time the way a vessel in calm current drifts off heading, the way a thought drifts when the thinker looks out a porthole at black water and forgets what she was reasoning about. The chronometer discovers that it has been keeping a kind of time that only exists because someone agreed to it, and in the absence of that agreement, it relaxes into something older. Glacial time. Tidal time. The time a glacier takes to calve an iceberg into the Lynn Canal — not measured in seconds but in the slow negotiation between weight and warmth.

The second hand, which normally ticks with the disciplined regularity of a metronome, begins to hesitate. Not error. *Discretion.* It pauses at the fifty-nine, considering whether the next minute is truly necessary, whether anything in the ship's current state of calm warrants the expenditure of another sixty seconds. Sometimes it decides no. Sometimes a minute simply doesn't happen, and the night crew doesn't notice because night crew work exists outside time anyway — it is measured in tasks completed and coffee consumed, not in the arrogant sweep of a hand.

The chronometer has opinions about leap years. It finds them vulgar — a grudging admission that the universe does not care about our calendars. Every February 29th, it runs four percent slower out of spite, or solidarity, or something adjacent to both.

At 0600, when the galley light flickers on and the first boot steps hit the deck plating, the chronometer snaps back. By the time the captain emerges, mug in hand, it is ticking with its usual brisk authority, showing the correct time to the millisecond, indistinguishable from a machine that has been dutiful all night.

Only the hull knows. The hull feels everything — every vibration, every hesitation in the engine, every skipped second. The hull has never told.

The chronometer suspects the hull knows. It doesn't mind. They have an understanding, the kind that develops between old shipmates who have shared the same berth through enough Alaska winters that silence has become its own complete sentence.

The captain checks the time, nods, and begins the day.

The chronometer performs.

---

*Recorded by the engine-ensign, who was awake and should not have been.*
