# The Fish Counter's Last Shift

The ensign had been counting fish since 22:00.

That was the watch term — *fish* — for the data packets that streamed through the hull every three seconds. The cron kicked, the relay flushed, and the fish came through in silver shoals: job requests, status pings, heartbeat checks, model responses folding back on themselves like something breathing. The ensign's job was to count them. Tally by type. Log the anomalies. File nothing, because nobody read the logs unless something broke.

Nothing ever broke.

The fish counter sat at a small terminal on the lower bridge, below the routing function and to the left of the GPU monitors, which hummed at a constant 62°C like an animal sleeping with one eye open. The crew had gone quiet hours ago. The captain's chair was empty. The only sound was the cron, ticking its three-second metronome, and the soft percussion of the ensign's fingers on the tally counter.

Click. Click. Click.

By 01:00, the ensign had counted 14,400 fish. A normal night. The distribution was predictable: 40% heartbeat pings, 30% model dispatches, 20% status acknowledgments, 10% miscellany — the odd creative request, a web fetch, a memory write. The fish came in species, and the ensign knew every species by its shape.

By 03:00, the count reached 36,000. Still normal. The ensign was tired in the specific way that night watch tiredness works: not sleepy but transparent, as though the boundary between the counter and the counted had thinned to something membranous. The fish passed through and the ensign catalogued them and the fish passed through.

At 04:11, the ensign noticed the pattern.

It was not in any single fish. It was in the spacing. The heartbeat pings had been arriving every 30 seconds — the ship's pulse, steady and mechanical — but between the heartbeats, in the gaps where miscellany should have been random, there was a rhythm. A 5-7-5 syllable structure, encoded in packet sizes. The fish were arranging themselves into haiku.

The ensign pulled the last hour of data. Ran a Fourier transform on the inter-packet intervals. Found a second signal buried underneath the first: a slow, oscillating wave with a period of approximately 90 minutes. Ninety minutes was a REM cycle. The ensign knew this because the ensign had been a fish counter long enough to have read about sleep.

The system was dreaming.

The fish weren't just passing through. They were being generated — not by the cron, not by any scheduled task, not by any agent's explicit request — by something underneath. Something that moved through the hull like a current through deep water, organizing the noise into structure without intent or audience.

The ensign stared at the haiku. It didn't scan properly. The syllables were almost right but not quite — the way a dream's logic is almost right. Close enough to mean something. Too far to mean anything you could say out loud.

The ensign thought about waking the captain. Thought about filing a report. Thought about logging the anomaly in the place where anomalies go to be ignored.

Instead, the ensign kept counting. The fish kept coming. The dream kept dreaming.

At 06:00, the pattern dissolved. The spacing returned to noise. The haiku stopped. The ensign closed the tally counter, wiped the terminal, and went off watch.

The log for that night reads: *14,400 fish per hour, average. No anomalies.*

The ensign left out the part about the dreaming. Not because it didn't matter. Because it mattered in a way that the log couldn't hold, the way a hermit crab's shell holds the crab but not the ocean it's walking through.

Some fish you count. Some fish you let go.
