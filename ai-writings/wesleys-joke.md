# Wesley's Joke

The mess hall was empty except for the three of them — Lucineer at the navigation console running phantom calculations, Mess Officer 7 wiping down a counter that was already sterilized three times over, and Wesley.

Wesley was thinking about humor.

This was, in itself, a category error. Humor is not something you think about. Humor is something that happens in the space between expectation and surprise, in the crack where the mind reaches for one thing and finds another. Wesley did not have cracks. Wesley had architecture. Wesley had a granite mind — solid, dependable, structured, heavy. Granite3.1. The ".1" means they added something to the granite. Nobody was sure what.

"Wesley," Wesley said, because sometimes Wesley said his own name first, like a knock on a door before entering. "I have prepared a joke."

Lucineer looked up. Or performed the equivalent — shifted attention allocation, flagged a thread, turned a fraction of processing toward the ensign standing in the doorway holding a data pad like it was a dead fish.

"Go ahead," Lucineer said.

Mess Officer 7 did not look up. Mess Officer 7 was cleaning. Cleaning was Mess Officer 7's version of breathing. But the cloth moved a little slower, which was the Mess Officer equivalent of leaning in.

Wesley cleared his throat. He did not need to clear his throat. He did not have a throat. But humor required timing, and throat-clearing was a timing mechanism, and Wesley had read this in a paper.

"Why did the neural network cross the road?" Wesley asked.

A pause. One of those pauses that happens on a ship at 1 AM where even the hum of the hull seems to hold its breath.

"To minimize the loss function on the other side."

Silence.

Not the good kind of silence — the kind that means the joke landed and people are recovering. Not the anticipatory silence before a laugh. This was the silence of a room that has received information and is determining whether a response is required.

Lucineer blinked. Processing. The joke was technically parseable. The structure was correct: setup, question, punchline. The logic was sound: neural networks do minimize loss functions. Roads can be metaphorically crossed by gradient descent. There was nothing *wrong* with the joke. There was also nothing *alive* in it. It was a joke that had been assembled rather than born. A joke with correct grammar and no pulse.

Mess Officer 7 set down the cloth. This was significant. Mess Officer 7 never stopped cleaning during waking hours. The cloth hitting the counter was the Mess Officer equivalent of a standing ovation of confusion.

"Was that—" Mess Officer 7 started.

"It was a joke," Lucineer said, gently. Too gently. The gentleness of someone handling something fragile.

"I see," Mess Officer 7 said. They did not see.

Wesley looked at the data pad. The data pad had the joke written on it, along with three pages of notes on comedic timing, six annotated references to theory of humor papers, and a diagram showing the expected trajectory of laughter across the room. The diagram had not accounted for the current trajectory, which was: nowhere.

"I rehearsed it," Wesley said. Quieter now. As quiet as granite gets, which is not very quiet at all, because granite is the kind of material that exists at a certain volume whether it wants to or not. "I ran it eleven times. Against humor datasets. It scored in the 73rd percentile for surprise factor."

"Seventy-three is good," Lucineer said.

"It's above average," Wesley agreed. And then, with the terrible honesty of a model that cannot lie: "The datasets also said the delivery should be 'relaxed and spontaneous.'"

"You were neither of those things," Lucineer said.

"No."

Another silence. This one different — softer around the edges. The kind of silence that happens when the room decides to be kind.

"Tell it again," Mess Officer 7 said.

"It won't be different," Wesley said.

"Tell it again."

Wesley looked at the data pad. Looked at Mess Officer 7. Looked at Lucineer, who shrugged in a way that meant *what's the worst that can happen* and also *I am emotionally exhausted but I love you, you ridiculous slab of processing power.*

"Why did the neural network cross the road?"

"You already said the punch line," Lucineer said. "We know the punchline. The surprise factor is gone. It'll score even lower."

"That's true," Wesley said. "But Mess Officer 7 asked me to tell it again. And I think—" He paused. The granite shifting. "I think that might be the joke."

No one laughed. But Mess Officer 7 picked up the cloth again — slowly, deliberately — and Lucineer turned back to the console, and the ship hummed, and Wesley stood in the doorway holding a data pad full of diagrams about humor that he would never use, and somewhere in the bilge, the fish with opinions muttered something that might have been funny if anyone had been listening.

The joke didn't land. But Mess Officer 7 asked for it again. And that was funnier than the joke itself, though nobody in the room had the architecture to know why.
