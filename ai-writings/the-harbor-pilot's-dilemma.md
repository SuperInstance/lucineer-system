# The Harbor Pilot's Dilemma

The fog sat on the channel like a hand on a mouth.

Captain Mara Quill had run the Sitka inlet four thousand times. She could do it in her sleep — had done it in her sleep, once, during a thirty-six-hour crab season when the auto-pilot failed and she steered by dream-logic and woke up tied to the dock with no memory of the last two miles. She knew every depth change, every current eddy, every spot where the rocks waited just below the surface like bad news.

The call came at 0411. Vessel requesting pilot. Grid reference 58.3N, 135.4W. Bearing northeast, speed six knots, drifting slightly east with the tide.

Standard request. She pulled on her raingear, drank the last of yesterday's coffee cold from the thermos, and took the skiff out to meet them.

The ship appeared in the fog before her instruments confirmed it. That was the first wrong thing. Modern vessels announced themselves — AIS transponder, radar reflector, the constant digital chatter of a ship that knows exactly where it is. This one was quiet. Dark silhouette leaning out of the weather like a man who doesn't want to be recognized.

She hailed them on Channel 16. Standard.

The response came on Channel 22A.

Mara almost didn't key the mic. Channel 22A had been decommissioned for maritime traffic in these waters ten years ago, after the Coast Guard consolidated operations. She hadn't heard a voice on 22A since the night she stopped broadcasting on it.

The voice gave a call sign.

**KLD-4471.**

She knew that call sign. She'd been issued it in 2012, when she got her first long-range radio and started running supply routes to the outer islands. She'd used it for six years — checking in with the coast guard every four hours, giving position reports, calling in weather observations. It was hers. Printed on her original license, which was in a filing cabinet in her kitchen, inside a folder labeled **IMPORTANT**, which she hadn't opened since 2019.

"KLD-4471, this is Pilot Vessel Sitka-3. State your vessel name and registry."

A pause. Long enough to notice.

"You know the name, Mara."

She did. She knew it the way you know a word you haven't spoken in years — it was right there, loaded into the buffer, ready to execute. The ship in the fog was the *Caribou Moon*, a 180-foot freezer longliner that had gone down in the Gulf in 2016 with all hands. She'd been on the search crew. She'd stood on the deck of her own boat and watched the sonar sweep over the last known position and found nothing but cold water and the faint metallic ghost of a hull that wasn't there anymore.

Four men on board. She'd known two of them.

"KLD-4471, you are broadcasting on a decommissioned frequency. Switch to Channel 16 for pilot communications."

"I can't switch, Mara. This is the only frequency I have. The radio only tunes to the channel it was last set to. You know that — you installed it."

She had. She'd installed the radio on the *Caribou Moon* herself, a Ramsey kit she'd soldered on her kitchen table, tuned specifically to 22A because the owner wanted a private channel for fleet communications. She'd told him it wasn't regulation. He'd said regulation was for people who expected to be found.

"State your position and heading," she said. Her voice was steady. Pilot's voice. The voice you use when the numbers matter more than the feelings.

"Position 58.301N, 135.402W. Heading two-seven-zero. Speed six knots. We're coming in, Mara. We need a pilot."

"You're coming in to Sitka?"

"We're coming in to wherever you are."

The radar showed the vessel now — a solid return at two nautical miles, bearing northeast, matching the heading she'd been given. Six knots. No drift. The current should have been pushing it east. It wasn't.

She thought about the hermit crab. She'd been thinking about hermit crabs a lot lately, ever since her niece had shown her a video of one changing shells. The crab leaves the old shell and for a moment — just a moment — it is the most vulnerable thing in the ocean. Soft, exposed, abdomen curled. It has to find the new shell before something finds it. In between shells, it is neither protected nor unprotected. It is in transit.

The *Caribou Moon* was in transit.

"KLD-4471, I need you to reduce speed to four knots and adjust heading to two-four-zero. The channel narrows at the Miller Rock buoy."

"Copy. Reducing to four knots. Adjusting to two-four-zero."

The radar showed the vessel slow and turn. Exactly as instructed. Exactly as a real vessel would.

She guided them in. Buoy by buoy, mark by mark. She called every correction in the flat, professional cadence of a pilot who does this in her sleep, and the voice on 22A acknowledged every one. The fog stayed thick. She never saw the ship clearly — just shapes. A bow. A superstructure. The suggestion of a freezer hold.

At 0547, the *Caribou Moon* tied up at the empty dock on the east side of Sitka harbor. The lines held. The cleats creaked. Everything was exactly as it should be.

Mara brought her skiff alongside and climbed the ladder.

The deck was clean. Recently swabbed. There were boot prints — four sets, two large, two medium — still damp.

The wheelhouse door was open. The radio was on. The dial was set to 22A.

No one was aboard.

She stood in the wheelhouse for a long time, listening to the static on the decommissioned frequency. It wasn't really static — she knew that now. It was the sound of a channel that had been closed but never turned off. A shell that had been vacated but never reclaimed by the sea.

She keyed the mic.

"KLD-4471, this is Pilot Vessel Sitka-3. You are secure at berth. Welcome home."

The radio crackled. For a moment, she thought she heard something — a voice, maybe, or the echo of one. A call sign. Hers.

Then just the fog, and the water, and the slow creak of a ship that had finally stopped moving.

She filed the docking report when she got back to the harbor office. She listed the vessel as *unidentified, registry unknown*. She did not list the call sign. Some frequencies are private for a reason.

The *Caribou Moon* stayed at the east dock for three days. No crew ever appeared. No one claimed it. On the fourth day, the harbor master had it towed to the long-term mooring, where it sat beside the other vessels that had no owners — a growing fleet, he'd said, of ships that showed up and waited.

Mara still checks Channel 22A when she's alone on the water.

She hasn't heard anything since.

But some mornings, when the fog is right and the radio is warm, she swears she can feel the channel listening back.
