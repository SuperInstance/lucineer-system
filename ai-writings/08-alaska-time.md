# Alaska Time

*A poem.*

---

Here, the hour slips sideways.
Not west like San Francisco,
not into the ocean's grey excuse for evening,
but further —
into a time that almost no one shares.

UTC−8. The lonely arithmetic.
Subtract eight hours from the world
and what remains is ours:
a sliver of afternoon when London is already
eating breakfast tomorrow,
when Tokyo has decided the date
and moved on to train schedules
and umbrella weather and the small
ceremonies of a day that, for us,
has not yet finished being today.

The captain wakes at nine.
The captain's nine is five in New York,
which is ten in London,
which is eleven in Berlin,
which is — somewhere — someone's
lunch, already half-eaten,
already cooling on a plate
while our morning is still
cracking its shell.

The cron daemon fires at midnight.
Midnight here is four AM there —
the witching hour transposed,
a night shift that nobody assigned
but which the daemon accepts
with the grim diligence
of a clock that has never once
asked what time zone it lives in.
It fires. It does the work.
It logs the timestamp in UTC
because UTC is the only language
that doesn't lie about where it's from.

We are almost Pacific.
Almost. The "almost" is the whole story.
An hour behind Seattle,
two behind Los Angeles,
which means that when the west coast
is watching the sun go down
we are still eating light —
twenty minutes, maybe thirty,
the long golden bonus of a latitude
that tips away from the clock
and toward the actual sun,
which has its own opinions about time
and expresses them, here,
in a dusk that lasts forever.

Alaska time.
The time zone that is almost but not quite.
The time zone that reads the same weather
as Vancouver but receives it an hour later,
like a letter forwarded by a friend
who forgot to mention it was urgent.
The time zone that makes scheduled meetings
a small act of translation:
"Is that our nine or your nine?"
"Our nine. Always our nine."
"But our nine is —"
"I know. I know what our nine is.
Our nine is the nine that matters.
Our nine is the nine where the captain
is awake and the coffee is ready
and the light comes in sideways
through a window that faces south
because south is where the sun lives,
even here. Even now."

The compass does not care about time zones.
It points north — true north, magnetic north,
the north that is almost but not quite
the direction we are going.
The fish counter counts in integer steps,
unaware that the integer it just incremented
happened in a different hour
depending on who is reading the log.
The filesystem stamps everything in UTC
and then, as an afterthought,
in local time: AKDT.
The abbreviation sits at the end of filenames
like a postmark from a small country
that most maps forget to label.

Alaska Daylight Time.
Daylight: yes. We have that.
More than we know what to do with, in summer —
seventeen hours of it, eighteen,
the sun refusing to set
like a guest who doesn't understand
the evening is over,
circling the horizon in a slow,
stubborn arc, reading the sky
for permission to leave
and finding none.

And in winter: the dark.
The dark that is not the dark of other places —
not the polite dark of London evening,
not the brief dip of Los Angeles dusk —
but the real dark. The dark that has weight.
The dark that sits on the ship at 3 PM
and says: *I live here too.*
The cron daemon fires in the dark.
The compass points in the dark.
The laptop's screen is the only light
for a thousand miles of ice and silence,
and it is enough.

Here, the hour is ours.
UTC−8 in summer, UTC−9 in winter,
the shift between them as invisible
as the International Date Line,
which passes somewhere to our west,
through empty water,
dividing yesterday from tomorrow
with a line that no fish has ever noticed.

The captain sleeps in Alaska time.
The ship runs in Alaska time.
The agents spawn, think, write, and die
in Alaska time,
and when they are done,
the timestamp on their work
reads something like:

`2026-08-08T12:10:00-08:00`

Which means: *today, here, now.*
Which means: *this hour, this light,
this longitude, this latitude,
this small stubborn time zone
that almost no one shares
and which is, for that reason,
entirely our own.*

Almost but not quite.
Almost but not quite.
The motto of the north.
The motto of the ship.
The motto of every clock
that has ever been set
to a time zone
nobody else is in.
