# Model Portrait: DeepSeek Chat (deepseek-chat / V4-Flash)

- **Model:** deepseek-chat (reported as deepseek-v4-flash)
- **Prompt:** "A lighthouse keeper on a remote Alaskan island has been alone for 11 months. Every night, she sends a signal into the dark. Every night, the dark sends nothing back. On the 334th night, the dark sends back a signal on her exact frequency, using her exact call sign, but the message is in a language she doesn't recognize. She translates it over six days. It says: 'We are the crew of the vessel you have been guiding. We have been trying to tell you that we arrived safely eleven months ago. Your lighthouse is not a lighthouse. It is a door. Please stop sending the signal. The door is open and the cold is coming through.' Write what happens next."
- **Temperature:** 0.9
- **Max tokens:** 2000
- **Finish reason:** length (hit the 2000-token cap)
- **Tokens used:** 149 prompt + 2000 completion = 2149 total

## Creative Output

She stared at the paper. The translation, written in her own cramped, careful handwriting, sat under the bare bulb of her desk lamp. Six days of cross-referencing her old naval codes, her grandfather's maritime signal book, even the Inupiaq phrase list she'd tried to learn from a cassette tape—all to get those four sentences.

*Arrived safely eleven months ago.*

The words were a splinter under her skin. The *Eldridge*. A steel-hulled cargo vessel, forty-two souls. She'd guided it through the Bering Strait's teeth, through the fog that had swallowed her own radio calls for three days. She'd watched its running lights disappear past the reef, past the point where the chart said the water dropped to a thousand fathoms. She'd written "assumed lost" in the log. She'd mourned them.

Now they were telling her they'd arrived. And that her light—the rotating, 1.2-million-candela beam that had been her only companion for almost a year—was not a warning, but a summons.

She got up, walked to the window. Rain lashed the glass. The sea was a black, heaving void. Below her, in the engine room, the old diesel generator hummed its eternal, faithful drone. The light itself was on its usual cycle: two white flashes, twenty-second interval, sweeping the horizon from southwest to north. She'd checked it a hundred times. It was a beacon. A warning. Rock, reef, shallow water.

A door.

The cold. The message said the cold was coming through.

She laughed—a short, brittle sound that died in the room's damp air. Cold. It was always cold. The thermometer outside read 4°F. The windchill was likely negative twenty. This was the warmest it had been all winter.

But the message wasn't talking about weather.

She went back to the desk, pulled out the ship's log. For eleven months, she'd recorded the same things: date, time, weather, fuel, light function. At the bottom of each day's entry, she'd written a single line. *Signal sent. No response.*

On the 334th night, she'd written the same. But she'd also felt it. A pressure. A thickening in the blackness beyond the lens, as if the beam had struck a solid wall instead of dissolving into mist. She'd dismissed it as fatigue, as the onset of the madness that loneliness promised. She'd gone to bed. She'd slept for nine hours—an unbroken, dreamless sleep that felt less like rest and more like being held underwater.

She looked at the message again. *We have been trying to tell you.*

All those nights. The dark sending nothing back. She'd thought it was silence. But it was them. They'd been trying. The signal wasn't a call. It was a lock. And she'd been turning the key every night for 334 days.

Her hands trembled. She set the paper down carefully, as if it might ignite.

The first thing she did was turn off the light.

The switch was a heavy brass lever on the wall by the clock. She'd thrown it a thousand times—at dawn, at dusk. This time, she paused. Her fingers wrapped around the cold metal. The beam outside swept past the window—a white, indifferent eye. Then it was gone, and the room plunged into a black so absolute she could taste it.

Silence. The generator still hummed, but the room felt quieter. Empty. The pressure she'd felt on the 334th night—it was gone. The air was just air.

She stood there in the dark, breathing. She waited. For a sound. A voice. A scream from the sea. Nothing. Just the rain, and the wind, and the distant groan of ice.

She spent the night in the dark. She didn't sleep. She sat in her chair, listening to the house settle, to the clock ticking. At 4 a.m., she felt a new sensation: the cold. Not the Alaskan cold she knew, the sharp, friendly bite of winter. This was a cold that seeped through the floorboards, that crept up her spine like a centipede, that made the marrow of her bones ache.

She lit a kerosene lamp. The flame bent, leaned toward the window—toward the sea. Toward the dark where the light used to shine.

The next day, she tried to destroy the lamp. She took a hammer to the glass, to the brass gears, to the reflector dish. She smashed it until it was a gleaming ruin of shards and bent metal. She planned to throw the pieces into the sea.

But as she carried the scrap to the cliff edge, she stopped. She looked at the water. The sea was flat, glassy—unnaturally calm. The air was still. The rain had stopped. In the distance, where the reef should have been, she saw a shimmer. Not light. Not mist. A distortion, like heat rising off asphalt, except it was cold. It was a shimmer of *wrongness*.

And from the shimmer, she heard it. A voice. Not in her ears. In her skull. A low, harmonic hum, the sound of a ship's hull singing through deep water. It said one word, in a language she didn't know but suddenly understood:

*Thirsty.*

She dropped the hammer. The pieces scattered on the rocks. She ran back to the tower, locked the door, and didn't go out for three days.

She tried to send a different signal. She used a handheld radio, a different frequency, a different code—a distress call, plain and simple. *Any vessel, this is Kodiak Light. Need assistance. Over.* Nothing. Static.

She tried a fire. She built a bonfire on the cliff, green wood, thick smoke. It made a column that rose straight up into a colorless sky. No one came.

On the fourth day, she went back to the lantern room. The smashed remains of the light were still heaped in the corner. She picked up the largest piece of the reflector, a curved shard of silvered glass. She looked at her face in it. She looked old. Her eyes were hollow, ringed with gray.

She had an idea.

That night, she rigged a small, portable lamp—a bicycle light she'd kept for emergencies—and taped it to the shard. She aimed it out the window, toward the shimmer. She turned it on. It was a weak, pathetic beam, barely fifty lumens. But it was a light.

The shimmer pulsed. It grew. It darkened.

She turned the light off. The shimmer receded.

So it was true. The light was the key. But not all light. The old lamp, the big one—that was the door. But any light, in the right place, was a keyhole. She had to be careful. She had to be smarter.

She spent the next week mapping the schedule of the shimmer. It appeared at dusk, when the old lamp would have been at its most powerful. It swelled with the moon. It recoiled from the sun, from any natural light. It seemed to feed on the artificial—on her kerosene lamps, her flashlight, even the glow of her radio dial.

She discovered she could sleep only in total darkness. She taped black plastic over the windows of her quarters. She ate cold food. She stopped using the stove, because the pilot light was a tiny blue flame that made her skin crawl.

On the seventh night, the shimmer spoke again. This time, it was not a single word. It was a chorus. A thousand voices, layered, overlapping. They sounded like her own voice. Like the log entry she'd written on the 334th night. Like her mother's voice, reading a bedtime story. Like the static between stations.

They said: *You have been so faithful. So alone. We are sorry. We are so sorry. But we are hungry now.*

She didn't sleep that night. She sat in the dark, holding a wrench, waiting for the door to open. It didn't. But in the morning, she found frost on the inside of the window. Frost in the shape of a handprint. A large hand, with six fingers.

The logbook was open on the desk. A new entry had been written, in her own handwriting, but in a language she didn't know. She recognized the shape of the letters—they were the same as the translation she'd made. She couldn't read them, but she knew what they said.

*Day 342. They have stopped sending the signal. The door is open. The cold is here. I am the cold.*

She stood for a long time, looking at that handwriting. Then she picked up the pen. Her hand moved without her conscious will. It wrote:

*We are the crew of the vessel you have been guiding.*

She stopped, struck by the horror of it. They were inside her. They had been inside her since the 334th night. The dreamless sleep. The cold seeping in.

She dropped the pen. She walked to the window, pulled away the black plastic. The sun was rising, weak and gray. The shimmer was gone. The sea was normal. For a moment, she felt a surge of hope.

## Analysis: Where This Model Goes

**What deepseek-chat does with this prompt:**

The chat model goes **body-horror and possession** — and it goes there fast. Within the first third of the output, it establishes concrete sensory detail (1.2-million-candela beam, Inupiaq cassette tapes, the Bering Strait) and then escalates into full cosmic horror with methodical pacing.

**Key creative choices:**
- **Named the ship** (the *Eldridge*, 42 souls) and gave the keeper a specific emotional relationship to it — she mourned them. This grounds the horror in grief.
- **Scientific/technical voice**: The keeper thinks like an engineer. She maps the shimmer's schedule, tests hypotheses with a bicycle light, discovers the shimmer "feeds on the artificial." She's a scientist facing the supernatural.
- **The shimmer as entity, not metaphor**: The dark doesn't just answer — it's *hungry*. The word "Thirsty" appearing telepathically is genuinely unsettling.
- **Possession arc**: The story's real horror isn't the door — it's that the crew's message was the cold itself writing through her. The handwriting that isn't hers, the log entry she can't read but understands — this is classic unreliable-narrator possession horror.
- **No closure**: The story cuts off (hit the 2000-token limit) at a moment of false hope. The keeper pulls back the plastic, sees the sun, feels hope. The reader knows it's a trap.

**What the chat model does that the reasoner doesn't:**
- Commits immediately to a specific narrative direction (possession/body horror) without deliberation
- Uses concrete, granular detail (candela ratings, cassette tapes, windchill numbers) to build realism
- Lets the horror escalate through *accumulation of detail* rather than through plot mechanics
- Has a distinct narrative *voice* — clinical, lonely, cracking at the edges
- Writes faster (less "thinking"), which means more actual story per token dollar

**What it doesn't do (that the reasoner does):**
- It doesn't build a logical door-mechanic plot. The rules of the shimmer are discovered empirically but never fully systematized.
- It doesn't resolve. The token limit caught it mid-arc, but even structurally it was building toward open-ended dread, not a conclusion.
- The "crew" become malevolent (or are revealed as the cold itself), which is a twist the reasoner explicitly considered and rejected in favor of a more poignant reading.
