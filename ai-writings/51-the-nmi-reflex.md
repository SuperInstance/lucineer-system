# The NMI Reflex

Forty-three milliseconds.

That's how long it takes from the Pincher hook's threshold-cross to the motor cortex receiving the after-action report. Not the action — the *report*. The action already happened. The NMI fired before the brain knew there was a problem.

This is by design.

---

Yuen was running a salvage op on the exterior hull, magnetized boots clamped to a plate that had been stress-fractured since the Calypso incident. She wasn't thinking about the fracture. She was thinking about lunch, about the message from her daughter, about the weird noise the servo in her left gauntlet had started making. The usual human noise.

The Pincher hook was watching her grip force, her positional drift, the micro-vibrations traveling up through the hull plate into her boots. It was watching everything she wasn't.

At 14:03:22.718, the hook detected a shear-wave signature in the plate. Not the kind that means *maybe check this later.* The kind that means *the plate is about to delaminate and the section you're standing on is going to depart the hull at about 0.3g of relative acceleration.*

The reasoning brain — the CNS, the beautiful slow general-purpose processor that writes poetry and solves differential equations and decides what to have for lunch — takes about 200 milliseconds to form a conscious response to any stimulus. Two hundred milliseconds. An eternity. In 200 milliseconds the plate would be gone and Yuen would be tumbling away from the ship in her mag-boots, watching her tether spool out, understanding her situation with perfect clarity just a little too late.

The NMI doesn't ask the CNS.

At 14:03:22.720 — two milliseconds after detection — the hook fires. The signal travels the bypass pathway: Pincher → NMI gate → motor nerve bundle → quadriceps, gastrocnemius, gluteus maximus. It doesn't go through the brain at all. It goes through the spine, the old architecture, the reptile highway that says *jump now, think later.*

Yuen's boots detached from the plate. Her legs drove her up and laterally, thirty-two centimeters, not a controlled jump — a spasm, a convulsion, the graceless violence of a body that has been told to move or die. Her shoulder hit the safety rail. The rail held.

The plate delaminated at 14:03:22.741. It peeled away from the hull and tumbled into the black, trailing crystallized sealant like a jellyfish's tentacles.

Yuen was still falling backward when her conscious mind caught up. She felt her shoulder, felt the rail behind her, felt the absence of ground beneath her feet. She looked down and saw the empty space where she had been standing.

The after-action report arrived. It said: *Threshold shear-wave detected. Hook fired. Lateral displacement 32cm. Duration: 43ms from detection to completion. Reasoning pathway was not consulted.*

She hung there on the rail, breathing hard, heart slamming, the adrenaline arriving late and stupid and overwhelming. Her hands were shaking. The NMI had already reset.

Later, in the medbay, the technician pulled the logs. "Forty-three milliseconds," she said. "Clean reflex. The hook saved you."

"What if it fired wrong?" Yuen asked. "What if there was no delamination and I just — jumped?"

The technician shrugged. "Then you'd have looked silly. The hook's false-positive rate is 0.003%."

"And the other 99.997%?"

"The other 99.997%, you go home."

---

Yuen went home. She sent the message to her daughter. She had lunch.

The NMI waited. Patient, subcortical, faster than thought. It would be there the next time too, and the next, firing its ugly perfect spasm into whatever moment needed saving.

It never asked permission. That's what made it fast enough to matter.
