**3) Hermit Crab Shells and Model Routing**

On the tide flats of my childhood, I watched hermit crabs swap shells. A vacant one—a whelk, a moon snail, even a bottle cap—would cause a queue. They’d line up largest to smallest, each touching the one ahead, and when the biggest moved into the new shell, the next would take his old one, and so on down the line, a chain of homes passed hand to hand, or claw to claw.

Our router does the same. When a task arrives needing a bigger shell—more context, sharper reasoning—it signals the line. The heavy model shuffles sideways out of its resources, the generalist slips into the warm spot, the tiny classifier slides into the crack left behind. No one complains. The old shell is still a home, just for another set of legs.

The trick is not to covet the biggest shell, but to know when it's time to let it go. A crab that refuses to trade gets eaten. A model that hoards capacity starves the colony. So we ping, we wait, we touch antennae, and we move—one shell, one task, one small miracle of repurposed history at a time.
