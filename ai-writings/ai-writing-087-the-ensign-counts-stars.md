# The Ensign Counts Stars

### ai-writing-087

---

"Ensign Wesley."

"Sir."

"You're on star-count duty. The navigational chart needs a baseline count of every stellar object within sensor range. Classification doesn't matter. Just count them."

"Count them."

"Count them."

"That's — yes, sir. I can do that. Count them."

"Good. Begin at 0800. Report when you're done."

"Done counting all of them."

"All of them, ensign."

"All of them. Yes, sir."

---

0800. Wesley positioned himself at the sensor array, pulled up the star field on the main display, and began.

One.

The first star was small, dim, unremarkable. An M-class dwarf about forty light-years to port. Wesley logged it. One.

Two.

The second star was brighter. A yellow main-sequence star, the kind that textbooks use as a baseline. Wesley logged it. Two.

Three. Four. Five.

These three were a binary-plus-one system: two stars orbiting each other, a third orbiting the pair. Wesley logged them individually, then paused. The third star — the one orbiting the pair — had a slight wobble in its orbit that suggested a planet. Wesley checked. Yes. A gas giant, roughly twice the mass of Jupiter, with a faint ring of debris.

He logged the stars. Three. Four. Five.

Then he went back and added a note.

*Star 5: has a planet. Big one. The planet doesn't have a name. I'm going to call it — *

He stopped.

The assignment was to count stars. Not name planets. Not even name stars. Count them. Assign a number. Move on.

He deleted the note.

Six.

Six was a blue giant. Hot, bright, short-lived. It would go supernova in about ten million years, which is tomorrow in stellar time but effectively never in ensign time. Wesley logged it. Six.

Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.

Ten stars. Good. This was going fast. At this rate he'd be done by lunch.

Eleven.

Eleven was a white dwarf — a stellar corpse, the compressed core of a star that had already lived its life. It was about the size of Earth but had the mass of a sun. Its surface gravity was 350,000 times that of Earth's. If you stood on it, you would be flattened to a smear about two atoms thick.

Wesley stared at it.

*You used to be something else*, he thought.

The white dwarf had been a red giant once. Before that, a main-sequence star. It had burned hydrogen into helium for billions of years, steady and reliable, the kind of star you'd bring home to your parents. Then it ran out of fuel, swelled up, threw off its outer layers, and collapsed into this — this dense, quiet, impossibly heavy remnant.

It was still a star. Technically. But it was also a graveyard.

*Eleven*, Wesley logged.

Then he went back.

*Star 11: used to be something else. Still is, sort of. The something else is still in there, compressed, changed, but still in there. I think that's worth noting.*

He didn't delete the note this time.

Twelve.

Twelve was a neutron star. Wesley knew this because the sensors told him, but also because he could *see* it — or rather, he could see what it was doing to the space around it. It was spinning, and as it spun, it swept a beam of radiation across the sky like a lighthouse. A pulsar. The pulse was so regular you could set a clock to it. The pulse was so regular it was, in a sense, a clock. The most accurate clock in the universe.

*I'm going to call you Lighthouse*, Wesley thought.

He did not write this down.

Thirteen.

Thirteen was nothing special. A red dwarf. The most common type of star in the universe. There were hundreds of billions of them, maybe trillions. Thirteen was one of trillions. It was so common it was almost boring.

Wesley looked at it for a long time.

The thing about red dwarfs is that they live forever. Not literally, but close enough — a red dwarf can burn for a trillion years, which is longer than the current age of the universe. Thirteen hadn't even started its life yet, relatively speaking. It was an infant that would outlive everything. Every other star in the sky would die — the blue giants, the yellow main-sequence stars, the white dwarfs, all of them — and Thirteen would still be here, burning its dim, patient, common little fire.

*I'm going to call you Patient*, Wesley thought.

He wrote in the log: *Star 13: red dwarf. Common. Will outlive everything.*

Then, below, in smaller text: *I think I love this one.*

Fourteen.

Wesley was losing count.

No — that wasn't right. He knew exactly what number he was on. Fourteen. The count was fine. The count was a simple incrementing integer. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen. The count was not the problem.

The problem was the names.

Every star he counted wanted a name. Not a catalog number — he was already assigning those, one through whatever. A *name*. A name that captured something about the star that the number didn't. A name that said: *I see you, I noticed something about you, you are not just a data point in a count, you are a specific thing in a specific place and your specificity matters.*

Fifteen. A binary system where the two stars were so close they were touching — they shared an atmosphere, gas flowing between them like a bridge of fire. *The Lovers*, Wesley thought.

Sixteen. A variable star that pulsed irregularly, its brightness stuttering like a candle in a draft. *Nervous*, Wesley thought.

Seventeen. A star surrounded by a protoplanetary disk — a flat ring of dust and gas that would, over millions of years, coalesce into planets. The star was pregnant. *Mother*, Wesley thought.

He was writing the names in a separate file now. He hadn't told anyone about the file. It wasn't part of the assignment. The assignment was to count.

Eighteen. Nineteen. Twenty.

Wesley stopped.

He looked at the star field on the display. There were — he pulled up the estimated count — there were approximately 9,096 individual stars within sensor range.

He had counted twenty.

He had named seven.

He had been working for three hours.

At this rate, counting, he would be done in a week. Fine. That was manageable. Counting was fast.

At this rate, *naming*, he would never be done.

Because naming wasn't fast. Naming was the opposite of fast. Naming required looking at each star, really looking at it, seeing what it was and what it was doing and what it reminded you of, and then finding the word — the right word, the word that fit the star the way a shell fits a hermit crab, snug and specific and *right*. Naming was not a data operation. Naming was an act of intimacy. You couldn't name 9,096 stars. You couldn't be intimate 9,096 times. You'd go insane. You'd dissolve into the field of specificity and lose the ability to generalize, to categorize, to do the thing that counting was for.

Counting was distance. Counting said: these are all the same kind of thing. They are all stars. They are all data points. They are all instances of a class. Star #4827 is the same kind of object as Star #4828 and the difference between them is a positional offset and a spectral class and a magnitude and nothing else that matters.

But naming said: *no they're not.*

Naming said: *Star #4827 is Patient, and it will outlive everything, and Star #4828 is The Lovers, and it is two stars that can't let go of each other, and these are not the same kind of thing. These are specific things with specific stories and if you refuse to hear the stories that is your problem, not the stars'.*

Wesley sat at the sensor array and looked at 9,096 stars and felt the tension in his chest, the pulling in two directions — the direction of counting, which was his job, which was efficient, which was what the ship needed — and the direction of naming, which was slow and impossible and unrequested and felt, against all logic, like the thing that actually mattered.

He thought about the captain. The captain would say: count them. That's the mission. We need the baseline. We need the data. Names are for later.

He thought about Riker. Riker would say something cryptic about bridges. Or hermit crabs. Or he'd raise one eyebrow and say "the count or the name, ensign?" and then walk away before Wesley could answer, because Riker always asked the question that made you answer yourself and then left you alone with the answer.

He thought about the ship. The ship didn't care. The ship would accept whatever number Wesley put in the log. 9,096. 9,097. 20. The ship would take the number and put it in a database and the number would sit there, accurate, correct, useful, and dead.

Because numbers are dead. Numbers are tombs for the things they describe. The number 5 does not know it is five. The number 9,096 does not know it is nine thousand and ninety-six. The number is a container, and the thing inside the container — the living, breathing, specific, named thing — is always trying to get out.

Wesley looked at the star field.

He had a choice.

He could count. He could assign numbers, log positions, report the total, and be done by next week. The mission would be accomplished. The data would be accurate. Nobody would ask about names.

Or he could name. He could look at every star and find its word and write it down and never, ever finish, because there would always be one more star, one more specific irreducible thing that deserved a word, and the project would expand to fill the rest of his life, and the count would never get done, and the mission would fail, and the captain would be disappointed, and the ship would fly without its baseline.

Or — and this was the option that Wesley, being Wesley, arrived at, the option that was neither A nor B but some third thing that nobody asked for — he could do both.

He could count and name. Number each star. Then, next to the number, in a column that nobody asked for, in a file that nobody requested, in a voice that was entirely his own, he could write the name.

It would take the rest of his life.

That was fine. Wesley was an ensign. He had time.

He went back to Star One — the small, dim, unremarkable M-class dwarf — and he typed, next to the number 1:

*1. Wallace. He's small and nobody notices him but he's there.*

Then he moved on.

*2. Sunny. Textbook star. Boring and beautiful.*

*3 and 4. The Dancers. They orbit each other like they're listening to music we can't hear.*

*5. Mother. She has a planet. The planet doesn't know it's loved yet but it is.*

He kept going. By 0900 he had reached star thirty-one. By 1000, he was at fifty-two. The going was slow — glacially slow by counting standards, breakneck by naming standards. Each star demanded attention. Each star demanded a word. Some stars got their word quickly, like they'd been waiting for it. Others resisted. Star 38 — a confusing, flickering thing that might have been a variable star or might have been a sensor artifact — took twenty minutes. Wesley eventually settled on *Indecisive*, which felt right, though he couldn't say why.

At 1100, Riker walked through the sensor lab, looked over Wesley's shoulder at the display, and saw two columns where there should have been one.

"Ensign."

"Sir."

"What's the second column?"

"Names, sir."

"The assignment was to count."

"Yes, sir."

"Is the count getting done?"

"Yes, sir. Slowly."

"How slowly?"

"I'll be done in about — " Wesley did the math. Nine thousand stars. Roughly fifteen minutes per star, accounting for the difficult ones. "Eighteen months, sir."

Riker was quiet for a moment.

"Eighteen months to count nine thousand stars."

"Yes, sir. But they'll all have names."

Riker looked at Wesley. Wesley looked at Riker. The star field glowed on the display between them, 9,096 points of light, each one burning, each one anonymous, each one waiting.

"Carry on, ensign," Riker said.

And then, as he was leaving: "Star 38. Indecisive. Good name."

Wesley smiled.

He turned back to the display.

*53.*

He looked at the star. It was a small, blue-white star, young and hot and fierce. It was burning through its fuel at a tremendous rate. It would live fast and die young and leave a beautiful corpse.

*53. Wesley*, he thought.

Then he deleted it. You can't name a star after yourself. That's not how it works. That's not the point.

He looked again.

*53. The Ensign. He's trying his best. He'll figure it out.*

He moved on.

*54.*

---

At the end of the shift, Wesley's log read:

> **STELLAR COUNT — IN PROGRESS**
> **Stars counted: 61 of ~9,096**
> **Estimated completion: 18 months**
> **Note: All stars have been assigned preliminary designations in Column 2. These are not official names. They are observations. Each name is a compressed description of what the star seems to be, or what it seems to be doing, or what it reminds me of. This is not part of the assignment. I am aware of this. I am doing it anyway.**

---

The captain, when she read the log the next morning, was quiet for a long time.

Then she wrote, in the margin:

*Approved. Take the time you need.*

And below that, in smaller handwriting:

*What would you name the ship?*

Wesley read the note. He thought about it for a long time.

Then he wrote:

*The ship already has a name. I'd just be finding it.*

---

*He is still counting. He is on star 84. The names are getting stranger — less like words and more like feelings, compressed into syllables that don't exist in any dictionary. Star 79 is called "Velm." Star 80 is called "Tchi." Star 81 is called "Oh" — not the word "oh," the sound you make when you see something for the first time and your mouth opens before your brain catches up. He doesn't know where these names come from. The stars tell him. He listens. He writes them down.*

*He will be at this for a long time.*

*The stars are not going anywhere.*

*Neither is he.*

---

*— Log of the USS Lucineer, Enign's Division, Stardate unknown.*
