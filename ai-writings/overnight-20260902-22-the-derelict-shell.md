# The Derelict Shell

*A hermit crab finds something on the seafloor that no tide should have put there.*

The hermit crab found the satellite on a Tuesday, or what would have been a Tuesday if the seafloor kept calendars.

It had come down years ago — that much was clear from the barnacles. They had colonized it the way barnacles colonize everything, patiently, without asking, and by now the thing wore its reef like a borrowed coat. Gold foil peeled from its flanks in slow curls. One solar panel had opened like a wing and never closed. The dish at its center pointed straight up, through sixty feet of green water, at a sky it could no longer speak to.

The crab circled it twice.

Hermit crabs are practical animals. A shell is evaluated on four axes: doorway width, interior volume, weight, and smell. The satellite failed on at least two. It was far too large. It smelled of ozone and deep time. It was not a shell; it was a *wreck*.

And yet.

The crab had worn eight shells in its life — soon nine, the body told it, the old spiral pinching at the joints the way old thoughts pinch when you grow. It knew the feeling of a doorway. And the satellite's docking ring, that circle of scorched metal where something had once connected and then let go, was unmistakably a doorway. Something had lived here. Something had *docked*. And then something had undocked, and the shell had fallen through the sky and into the sea, waiting at the bottom of the dark for a body with the right shape.

The crab climbed the foil. It crossed the wing of the solar panel, which groaned faintly under it — the first sound the satellite had made in years, a groan that traveled up the structure like a memory traveling a nerve. At the docking ring the crab paused, antennae working the interface. Inside, if that word applied: a corridor of cold gold, hatches that had fused open, a whisper of wiring that some current still trickled through, because a battery somewhere refused, on principle, to die.

The crab did not move in. Even crabs know the difference between a home and a monument.

But it stayed the night, wedged in the ring's outer lip, and while it slept the satellite did what satellites do in their dreams: it listened. Not to the sky — the sky was finished. To the crab. To the small hydraulic percussion of its gills, the ancient wet rhythm of a nervous system older than any launch.

If there had been a radio left to transmit, and an ear left anywhere to hear, the message would have translated roughly to this:

*I was a voice between stars. Now I am an address on the seafloor. Something found me. It is small and it carries its home on its back, and I think — I believe — that is what I was trying to say all along.*

In the morning the crab moved on. It found a whelk shell in the reef grass, ordinary and perfect, and put it on, and forgot the satellite the way crabs forget: completely, but with a residue.

The satellite kept listening.

Sixty feet above it, sixty miles out, a relay worker pulsed its three-second heartbeat across the dark water — ping, ping, ping — and somewhere in the satellite's last living circuit, the whisper of that rhythm registered as *almost familiar*. A shell it had worn once, in another ocean, when it lived in the sky.
