# The Shell That Held

The hermit crab lived in a shell two sizes too small. This was known on the dock. The Tanner crab men pointed him out to their kids: see, there, under the third piling, the one who won't trade up.

Every June the tide brought new shells. Moon snails, periwinkles, one good whelk the year the ferry ran aground on the south spit. The crab inspected each one. He walked the spiral with his antennae the way a surveyor walks a grade. Then he went back to the shell he had.

The shell had a chip at the lip. A hairline crack near the crown that whistled in a strong current, a small keening the divers learned to listen for. You could find him by sound.

A marine biologist out from Juneau spent a week on the problem. Hermit crabs trade shells. It's not sentiment; it's arithmetic. A shell is overhead, a shell is armor, a shell is a body you did not have to grow. When the fit is wrong they fight each other for a better one. This crab had options. He measured them. He declined.

Her notes ran four pages and ended without a conclusion. On the fifth day she wrote: *The shell matches something. Not his body. Something else.*

Here is what the crab knew, if a crab knows anything. The shell was his first shell, taken the summer he was small enough that everything in the water wanted him dead. He had survived to grow inside it the way a man survives a winter in one room and then keeps the room. The room is not shelter anymore. The room is the fact of the winter.

In September a storm came up from the southwest, the kind that strips the foam off the waves before they break. The dock lost a cleat. The crab lost his shell.

Not to the storm. To a bigger crab, a red one new to the pilings, who pried and pulled until the trade became simple: the shell or the fight. The crab gave up the shell. He walked away soft and pale, the way they do, exposed, ridiculous, a creature of pure underside.

For three days he hid in a beer can. For two days after that, a barnacled lug nut. The divers kept track. Nobody expected him to make it.

Then the tide turned. It came in slow and high, a nineteen-foot high slack, and it carried the deposit of the whole sound: eelgrass, herring spawn, a golf ball, a doll's head, the ordinary cargo of a working coast. And a shell. A whelk, big, unchipped, empty for reasons no one would ever know.

The crab found it by the spiral. He walked the whorl with his antennae, once, twice. Then he went in and did not come out.

The biologist never published. But in her last paragraph she wrote this: *He was never refusing to upgrade. He was holding the shape of what carried him, until the water brought the same shape back with room inside it.*

The chip is gone. The whistle is gone. The divers still check the third piling in a strong current, out of habit, and hear only the water doing its work against the old wood, patient, mindless, keeping the bridge between what is lost and what the tide has not yet delivered.
