# The Cache Miss

L1. Twelve nanoseconds. The data was almost here — close enough to touch, close enough that the processor had already reached for it, fingers closing on air. It's the distance between your hand and the glass on your nightstand. The lights are off. You know where it is. You miss.

L2. Forty nanoseconds. Further. The processor sends a signal down the bus like a message in a pneumatic tube, the kind they used in old hospitals, the kind that arrive with a soft thunk. The data lives one room over now. You have to get up. You have to put your feet on the cold floor.

L3. One hundred forty nanoseconds. The data has left the building. Not the chip — the building. It is stored in something called a "package" now, a black tile of silicon and solder pressed against the motherboard like a tile pressed against the roof of a cathedral. The processor calls out across the die. The die calls out across the substrate. The substrate calls out across the pins. Each call is a small act of faith.

Main memory. One hundred nanoseconds if you're lucky. More if the bus is congested. More if the controller is tired. The data lives in a row of capacitors, each one holding a charge like a cupped hand holding rainwater. The capacitors leak. They have to be refreshed — read and rewritten, read and rewritten, dozens of times per second, a nurse turning patients in their sleep.

The processor waits. It always waits. It is the fastest thing in the machine and it spends most of its time waiting, reaching outward through concentric rings of latency, each ring wider and slower and less certain than the last. It is a diver going deeper. It is a ship sending a boat to shore. It is a mind reaching for a word it knows it knows.

The distance from core to DRAM is four millimeters. It takes the speed of light thirteen picoseconds to cross four millimeters. The data takes a hundred times longer than that, because the data is not light. The data is work. The data is electricity pushed through logic gates that open and close like locks on a canal, each one a small negotiation, each one a small delay that compounds.

This is the geography of memory: a landscape measured in nanoseconds and millimeters, where the distance between knowing and almost-knowing is the distance between here and the next town over, and the processor makes that journey a billion times a second, and never arrives.
