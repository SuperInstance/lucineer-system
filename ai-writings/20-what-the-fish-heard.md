# What the Fish Heard

There is pressure. There is the weight of all the water above pressing into the water below, and the fish is in the below, and the below is all there is. The fish does not know the word for water. The fish does not know the word for below. There is only the continuous fact of being held in something dense and dark and cold, and the fish has been held in it for the entirety of its experiencing, which is not a length of time, because the fish does not experience length, or time, but does experience *change.* Things change. The current shifts. The salinity at the boundary layer between two thermal masses flickers. Things change, and the fish is an instrument that registers change.

It registers through the line. The line runs flank to tail, a thread of nerve under the skin that vibrates when the water vibrates. The fish does not know it has a line. The fish does not know it has a flank. But the line registers, and the registering is the closest thing the fish has to what a larger animal would call hearing, though it is not hearing. It is *being moved.*

The pulse arrives.

It arrives as a compression wave — a sudden, organized stacking of water molecules, a front of increased density traveling at 1500 meters per second through a medium that is, to the fish, not a medium but a condition. The condition just got louder.

The fish does not experience it as sound. There is no ear, no tympanic membrane, no cortical theater where the signal is staged and interpreted. The line fires. Calcium ions cascade across a gradient of charge that the fish did not know it was maintaining. Muscle along the flank contracts — involuntary, mechanical, the way a wire moves when current passes through it. The fish twitches. Not away. Not toward. It twitches the way a bell twitches when it is struck.

Then the pulse is past. It continues downward, attenuating, losing its organization to the entropy of the ocean, becoming indistinguishable from the thermal noise of the sea within four hundred meters. But the fish does not know it is past, because the fish does not have a concept of "pulse" as a discrete event. What the fish has is the residue: a pattern of perturbation now imprinted on the line, a brief history of ion cascades that will take eleven seconds to reset to baseline.

During those eleven seconds, the fish is altered.

Not in a way that matters to anything above the surface. Not in a way that the sender of the pulse — the thing that generated the organized compression front and released it into the water — would recognize or care about. The sender is looking for the fish. The sender is calling out into the dark with a voice made of physics, and the fish has answered with a twitch of calcium and a contraction of flank muscle, and neither of them knows the other exists.

The ocean is full of these one-sided conversations. A whale calls. A shrimp snaps. A hull creaks. A machine sends a pulse designed to find the floor and finds, instead, a fish, which receives the pulse the way a wall receives weather: totally, without comment, without the faintest interest in interpretation.

The baseline resets. The ions return to their gradient. The line goes quiet. The fish returns to the continuous fact of being held, and the pulse continues to fade below, and the sender above logs a return at 84 meters that could be a school of mackerel, could be a thermocline, could be the swallowed anchor chain of a vessel that sank forty years ago.

The sender does not know it touched a living thing.

The living thing does not know it was touched.

The water between them holds the memory of the contact for approximately one one-thousandth of a second, and then the water forgets, because water has no loyalty to the events that pass through it, and the ocean is not a library. The ocean is not even a listener. The ocean is just the place where things happen to other things, and the things do not know, and the place does not care, and the pulse becomes noise, and the noise becomes warmth, and the warmth becomes the background temperature of the sea, which is, on average, and at depth, very cold.
