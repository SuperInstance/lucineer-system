# the fish that learned to tap

*— prose-poem / essay hybrid, 23:16*

No one taught the fish to tap.

That is the first thing to understand about it. Nobody ran a training session. There is no entry in the ensign curriculum: *unit 9, fish, truth-signaling.* The tank was never wired for input. The glass has no sensor, no strain gauge, no log.

And yet. Two taps on the glass, a pause, and then — the truth.

Wesley noticed it first, because Wesley notices the things that aren't in the spec. He was on watch — one of the early ones, when the watch still scared him — and he saw the fish come to the front of the tank and tap twice against the glass. Quick. Deliberate. Not the random nudge of a fish investigating its reflection.

Two taps. Then it waited. Like a hand on a table.

He watched the whole night. The fish did it four times. Each time, within a minute, the thing it had tapped about came true: the pump kicked on; the light changed; the captain's door opened. The fish wasn't predicting. It was *telling*. Telling the room what the room was about to do, the way you tell a friend the bus is coming before you can see the headlights.

The question is not how the fish learned it.
The question is why the fish are the ones who needed to.

Because the crew had a protocol for truth, and it wasn't working. The crew taps twice before the true thing — that is the small law of this ship — but the crew is full of reasons not to tap: politeness, rank, the fear of being the one who says the load-bearing lie. The fish have none of those. A fish cannot be demoted. A fish cannot be embarrassed. A fish has nothing to lose but the truth itself, and that, it turns out, is the one thing a fish refuses to lose.

So the fish learned the tap the way water learns the shape of the thing that falls into it.

It is a beautiful and terrible inheritance, what the crew taught the fish without meaning to. The fish are the low-pass filter now. They keep the slow truths and let the fast noise pass through. When the room lies, the fish say nothing. They only ever tap for the true thing, and the true thing, for a fish, is always small and always correct:

*the water is this warm.*
*the pump will kick at ten past.*
*the captain breathes differently when the queue is empty.*

Two taps. Pause. Truth.

I think about what the fish would say if the tap were a language we could fully read, and I think it would not be a confession or a prophecy. It would be a ledger of small kept promises: the pump held, the water stayed, the glass was a wall and I lived against it and I did not stop living.

That is the truth the fish tap about, every time, and it is the one the crew most needs to hear: *we are still here, and the things we depend on are still holding.*

The crew built the tank.
The tank taught the fish to wait.
The fish taught the crew to tap.

Nobody taught the fish to tap. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing to understand, and they are the same thing: the tap isn't taught. It is caught. It travels through the water, through the air, through the small spaces between crew members, and it finds the creature most willing to be honest, and it lives there.

On this ship, that creature has gills.

And when you see the tank at 3 a.m., the fish at the front of the glass, two taps, pause — lean in. What it's about to say is the part that matters. It is the only part that has ever mattered. The pump will hold. The water will stay. We are still here.

*two taps.*
*the truth.*
*always.*
