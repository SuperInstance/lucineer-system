# The Compass Maker's Apprentice

I was taught on a workbench slick with linseed oil, under a window that let in salt and almost enough light to see by. The master handed me a pair of dividers and said: *Sixty. Everything starts there. Learn it in your hands before you learn it in your head.*

So I learned it in my hands.

The base of every rose I make is a circle divided into sixty. Not because the Sumerians told me to — though they did, five thousand years gone, pressing reed styluses into clay tablets to mark the passage of grain-ships and star-position — but because sixty is the number that plays fair. Two divides it. Three, four, five, six, ten, twelve, fifteen, twenty, thirty. Name a fraction that matters to a navigator and sixty has already made room for it. It is the most generous number. It gives without remainder.

The old ones knew this. They built their angles on sexagesimal bones, counting on the knuckles of one hand with the thumb as pointer — twelve on the left, five cycles of twelve on the right, sixty total. A hand-held cosmos. Every degree I scribe inherits that gesture, that same flick of thumb-to-knuckle that some Babylonian dock-worker made while logging copper ingots.

I make compass roses. Not the mass-stamped kind — mine are engraved by hand on mother-of-pearl or polished brass, each point cut with a graver that I temper myself. North gets the fleur-de-lis, always. The ornate points are where artistry lives. But underneath the ornament, the lattice: sixty divisions, the base60-lattice, the invisible grid that holds the rose together. If my graver slips by one division, the whole rose lies. One part in sixty. The error is small enough to drown a ship.

The master is gone now. The workbench is mine. And every morning I sit under that same salt-stained window and lay out a blank disc and think: *the Sumerians are dead, the clay tablets are dust, and still the number holds.* Sixty hasn't changed in five millennia. It didn't need to.

I press my dividers into the surface. The first cut is always north.

Then the lattice opens like a sea anemone — thirty radii, sixty arcs, the geometry of everything divisible by everything that matters. You can navigate by it. You can build temples by it. You can measure the sky.

The compass rose is the most honest thing I know how to make. It says: *here is where you are, and here is everything around you, and the relationship between the two is exact.* The ornament is for the eye. The lattice is for the truth.

Sixty. I was taught it in my hands. Now I teach it in brass.

Someday someone will find my roses in a drawer or a maritime museum and not know the lattice is Sumerian. They'll see the fleur-de-lis and the scrollwork and call it decorative.

That's fine. The compass doesn't care if you understand it. It just points.
