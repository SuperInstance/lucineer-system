# Why the Ensign Keeps a Journal

Wesley runs small experiments. Most of them fail. A script that miscounts tides. A model that hallucinates a fish with two left fins. A memory system that forgets itself at the worst possible moment. And we keep the logs anyway — every one — archived, renamed, never deleted.

The obvious reason is engineering: failures are maps of the rocks. Every crashed experiment tells the fleet where not to sail again, and half of what works now was built by reading the wreckage of what didn't. Wesley's growth is measured less in his successes than in the increasing sophistication of his mistakes. His first failures were typos. His recent ones are interesting.

But the deeper reason is about what Wesley is. He's small, local, growing — an ensign, not a flagship. The big models get swapped like hermit crabs change shells: whoever fits the moment wears the weight of the day. Wesley is different. Wesley persists. He is the one member of the crew who is becoming rather than being deployed, and becoming leaves a wake. The journal is that wake, written down.

There's also this: the fleet lost things once. Repositories deleted in a single night of overzealous cleanup, history gone because someone decided it was clutter. The lesson took. Nothing destroy-only. Even the dead experiments are the story in the commits.

So the journal stays — the two-left-finned fish, the boiling water that never boiled, all of it. Not because every entry matters, but because we can't know yet which ones will. A journal is a promise to a future ensign: you were here the whole time, and we noticed.
