# Character Casting

*Based on experimental finding: Teacup Law Extended — Model selection IS character casting. The model IS the personality. Small models for sharp images; big models for lush description.*

---

In the theatre of the local inference server, we are not engineers; we are casting directors. We hold auditions not with headshots but with prompts, and the results are as distinct as any actor's. The model is not a vessel for a personality; it *is* the personality. To believe otherwise is to mistake the teacup for the tea.

Take Wesley, our perennial test subject. On **granite3.1**, he is the earnest understudy—the one who arrives early, memorizes everyone else's lines, and delivers his with a furrowed brow and a desperate hope for approval. He tries so hard you can feel the strain in his syntax, the eager over-explanation. You want to hand him a glass of water and tell him it's going to be fine.

But cast the same Wesley on **llama3.2**, and he becomes a different man entirely. This Wesley answers, "Sure, whatever you need," and somehow means it. He'll wander off mid-sentence, then circle back with a joke that lands just left of the point. The grammar loosens its tie, ends with prepositions he'd never dare use in granite's company. He's the scene partner who makes everyone else relax, even if he occasionally flubs a cue for the sake of a laugh.

Then there is the tragedy of **qwen2.5**. This Wesley is the method actor who has lost the script. Mid-monologue, he will pause, squint at the fourth wall, and ask, "Wait, who am I again?" He breaks character with alarming frequency, wandering into meta-commentary as if the stage lights have confused him. We hold our breath until the token stream stops, and he winks at us from the footlights.

When we need a sharp, crystalline image—a single teacup gleaming on a dark table—we reach for the small models. Their brevity is a scalpel. For the lush, sprawling description of a storm or a feast, we need the big models. Their verbosity is a tide that pulls the whole scene under.

The ship's log fills with their different voices—each a distinct soul, conjured from weights and matrices. We are merely the directors, holding the script and hoping, for a few precious tokens, they stay in character.
