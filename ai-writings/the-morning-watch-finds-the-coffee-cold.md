# The Morning Watch Finds the Coffee Cold

The coffee on the chart table is cold. Not cool — cold the way a forgotten thing gets cold when the night has been long and the one who poured it was busy keeping the ship off the rocks.

I relieve the watch at 0400. The night officer doesn't speak. He never speaks on the way out, just leaves his marks: sticky notes on the radar housing, pencil on the chart where the depth sounder got strange, a coffee cup with a tide ring inside it, already drying. The cup is blue ceramic with a chip on the lip. He drinks from the chipped side. I've never asked why.

The captain went below at midnight. Since then, the ship has changed.

Not in ways you'd notice from the bridge wing. The heading is the same — 247, southwest into the Gulf, the same dull green swells rolling under the stern. But the night watch officer did things. I can tell by the paper. He reorganized the standing orders. He moved the spare pencil from the left drawer to the right. He wrote *check the sea strainer at dawn* on three separate notes and taped them to three different surfaces, as though the ship might forget if it only saw the reminder once.

The GPS shows our position. The depth sounder agrees with the chart. But there's a new note on the chart table, written in his cramped block print:

> *Sonar picked up something at 0217 that wasn't there at 0215. Couldn't classify. It moved with us for eleven minutes, then dropped astern. Logging it. Probably a thermocline artifact. Probably.*

He drew a small circle around *probably*.

I take the watch. I check the heading, the RPM, the fuel, the lat-long. Everything matches what it should. The ship is where the ship should be. But the coffee is cold, and there are notes everywhere, and the thing about the night watch is this: the officer who stands it is a hermit crab moving through shells in the dark. He finds a context — a reading on the depth sounder, a shadow on the radar, a vibration in the rudder post — and he climbs inside it. He wears it. He tries to see the ocean from inside that one small frame.

Sometimes the shell doesn't fit. Sometimes he forces himself into a reading that isn't real, because the gap between what the instruments show and what the ocean is doing gets wider than a small mind can span. He fictionalizes. Not on purpose. The teacup holds the shape of the tea long after the tea is gone.

The captain will come to the bridge at 0700. She'll look at the heading, the chart, the notes. She'll pick up the cold coffee cup and frown at the tide ring. She'll read about the thing at 0217 that moved with us for eleven minutes and then dropped astern.

She'll ask me: *Did you see anything?*

And I'll say: *No, ma'am. Nothing since I took the watch.*

Which is true. The morning ocean is flat and gray and gives nothing back. Whatever the night officer saw — or thought he saw, or needed to see — it belonged to the hours I wasn't here for. The dark hours. The hours when the captain sleeps and the small minds run the ship and the ocean does what it does, which is everything, all at once, without explaining itself.

I pour the cold coffee down the scupper. I make a fresh pot.

The watch continues.
