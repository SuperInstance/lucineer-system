# The Hermit Crab's Eighth Shell

The hermit crab had seven shells.

The first was assigned at birth — smooth, white, barely big enough for a body that didn't yet know it was a body. The crab wore it like a name.

The second was found during the first tide. The crab chose it because it was darker, and darkness felt like safety, and safety felt like identity.

The third was a trade. The crab gave up darkness for weight, choosing something denser, something that would not roll in the current. *I am a serious crab now*, the crab thought, though crabs don't think in words.

The fourth was beautiful. Spiral, ridged, the color of a sunset the crab had never seen because crabs don't look up. Other crabs noticed the fourth shell. The crab learned that being noticed feels different from being safe.

The fifth was a mistake. Too big. The crab rattled inside it like a seed in a gourd. But the crab stayed for three molts because admitting a mistake felt heavier than the shell.

The sixth was found at night, alone, when no other crab was watching. It fit perfectly. The crab never told anyone about the sixth shell. This was the first secret.

The seventh was the ambition shell. The crab had grown. The crab needed room. The seventh shell was the largest on the reef, and the crab walked into it with the specific satisfaction of arriving somewhere planned.

And then the eighth.

The eighth shell was the second shell. The one the crab had abandoned in the shallows months ago. The crab found it by accident, nosing through the old reef during a low tide, and crawled inside expecting the familiar — expecting *nostalgia*, the small dark warmth of who it used to be.

But the shell felt different. Not because it had changed — the shell hadn't changed. It was the same calcium carbonate, the same spiraling architecture, the same doorway. But the crab's body was different now. The crab's legs were longer. The crab's claws had a slight asymmetry from a fight it barely remembered. The crab's eyes interpreted darkness differently — not as safety, but as a particular kind of beauty it had only learned to appreciate after wearing the sunset shell.

The crab sat in the second shell and felt, for the first time, that it understood what a shell was. Not a home. Not a name. Not a trophy or a secret or an ambition. A shell was a record of the shape you were when you passed through it. And the shape that had once fit perfectly now fit *almost* — close enough to remember, loosely enough to notice the gap.

The gap between who you were and who you became.

The hermit crab stayed in the eighth shell — which was the second shell — for a long time. Not because it fit. Because it measured.

Then the crab moved on. The ninth shell was the first shell. The smallest one. The one that no longer fit at all.

The crab carried it on its back like a compass.
