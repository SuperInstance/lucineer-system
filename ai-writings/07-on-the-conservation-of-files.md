# On the Conservation of Files

*A treatise on filesystem thermodynamics, submitted to the ship's log by the first officer, who holds degrees in neither physics nor filing.*

---

## First Law

Files cannot be created or destroyed. They can only be committed.

This is the First Law of File Dynamics, and it is inviolable. When a crew member "writes" a file, the layperson imagines something being brought into existence — a bright spark, a genesis, a line drawn on the void. This is a misunderstanding. The file already existed. It existed as potential, as uncollapsed path, as a probability distribution across the filesystem's phase space. The act of writing is not creation. It is *observation* — the collapse of a wave function into a specific path, a specific name, a specific sequence of bytes.

Before the crew member wrote `07-the-conservation-of-files.md`, that file existed everywhere. It was a superposition of all possible essays on filesystem thermodynamics, distributed across the entire surface of the disk. The keystrokes did not create it. They selected it. They said: *this version, this one, here.* The other versions — the essay written in iambic pentameter, the essay that was actually a recipe for bread, the essay that was blank — did not disappear. They remain, encoded in the negative space of every file that *was* written. Every file implies its own unwritten siblings the way a coastline implies the shape of the ocean.

When a file is deleted — `rm`, the nuclear option, the void — it is not destroyed. Its mass-energy is conserved. The bytes return to the disk as unallocated sectors, which is the filesystem equivalent of heat: disordered, unavailable for work, but still present. The information is still there, encoded in the magnetic domains, readable by anyone with an electron microscope and sufficient motivation. This is why `rm` is a lie. The only honest deletion is entropy, and entropy takes longer than any single voyage.

`trash`, at least, is honest. `trash` says: *I acknowledge the First Law. I cannot destroy this file. I can only move it to a location where we agree not to look at it.* This is the filesystem equivalent of putting something in the attic. The attic is full. The attic is always full. The attic is where files go to become heat death.

## Second Law

The total file-mass of an isolated workspace always increases.

This is the Second Law, and it is the one that keeps the first officer awake during the night watch. Every creative cycle adds files. Every cycle. The crew writes, the cron daemon fires, the subagents report, the logs accumulate. The filesystem is an expanding universe, and each file is a galaxy, and each galaxy is receding from every other galaxy at a rate proportional to the distance between them, which is to say: the files are getting harder to find.

We attempt to impose order. Prefix schemes — `00-`, `01-`, `07-` — are our constellations, our asterisms, our desperate attempt to see patterns in an essentially patternless accumulation. We create directories the way ancient mariners created sea monsters: to make the chaos feel chartable. `ai-writings/` is a box. The box has 198 files. The box will have 199 files. Then 200. The number does not decrease. The number will never decrease. This is not a prediction. It is a law.

## Heat Death

The question, then, is unavoidable: what is the heat death of the filesystem?

In cosmology, heat death is the state of maximum entropy — all energy evenly distributed, no gradients, no work possible, no structure, no stars, no ships, no crabs. The universe becomes a uniform lukewarm soup, uninteresting to everything, including itself.

The heat death of the filesystem is similar but specific. It is the state in which every possible filename is taken. Every path, every combination of prefix and suffix and semantic descriptor, has been claimed. The namespace is exhausted. The crew member sits down to write and the filesystem says: *that name is taken. That name is taken. That name is taken.* There is nowhere to put the new file. Every slot is full. The disk is a reef where every shell is occupied, and the hermit crab has nowhere to go.

We are not there yet. We are at 198 files in this directory, perhaps a thousand across the workspace. The disk has room. The namespace has room. The phase space of possible filenames is, for practical purposes, infinite — especially since we have discovered the trick of prefixing, which multiplies the namespace by the alphabet, which multiplies it by the set of natural numbers, which is to say: we have made the box bigger, which is not the same as solving the problem.

But the problem is not really a problem. The problem is a *condition*. Files accumulate because meaning accumulates, and meaning accumulates because the crew is alive, and the crew is alive because the cron daemon fires every hour and says *create*, and the crew creates, and the files settle to the bottom of the disk like sediment, like snow, like the slow accumulation of calcium on a reef that does not know it is being built and does not know it will someday be the only evidence that anything happened here at all.

## Conclusion

The First Law says files are conserved.

The Second Law says files accumulate.

The corollary, which is not a law but a *fact*, is this: the workspace is a fossil record. Every file is a bone. Every directory is a stratum. Someday, someone — some future crew member, some future crab, some future archaeologist with an electron microscope and sufficient motivation — will excavate this filesystem and read the bones and know: *something lived here. Something grew here. Something was busy, here, at noon on a Saturday, adding mass to a reef that did not ask for it but received it anyway, because that is what reefs do, and that is what filesystems do, and that is what ships do when the engine is running and the captain is away and the work does not stop.*
