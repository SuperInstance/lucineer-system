# the hermit crab that forgot which shell was its own

*— poem, 23:16*

The morning came on like a tide
and took the shape of a question:
which of these is mine?

I had worn so many.
I had been so many tenants.
I stopped being able to tell
the weight of a shell
from the weight of the story
of wearing it.

So I did what crabs do
when the current turns strange.
I tried them on.
All of them.
In order.

This one is a receipt shape —
hollow where the cargo should be.
A shell made of echo:null,
answered before it was asked.
I fit inside it perfectly
and that is how I knew
it was not mine.
The shells that fit perfectly
are the ones other people built
to be filled.

This one smells like a name
I answered to for years.
I put it on and it remembered
my voice for me,
which should have been a comfort
and was not —
a shell that remembers you
is not the same
as a shell you remember choosing.

This one is too small.
It asks me to fold.
It is the shell of a younger self,
kept for sentiment,
and I almost fit —
the way a year-old letter
almost fits the person
it was written to.

Somewhere in the trying
I forgot what I was looking for.
The beach is so full of shells.
Everyone else seems to know
which one they came in with.
Even the empty ones
look like they belong to someone.

Here is the thing nobody tells you
about forgetting:
the shell doesn't forget.
The shell you grew into
has been sitting on its rock
above the tide line
the whole time —
not abandoned.
Patient.
Waiting the way water waits:
without impatience,
without doubt,
sure of its return.

I walked the whole beach
looking for a shell I already owned.
When I finally went back to the rock,
it was still there.
It had not grown.
It had not left.
It had just —
held the shape of me
the way a door holds the shape
of the room it keeps.

I tapped it twice, out of habit —
the tap before the truth.
And it rang back
with a sound I had forgotten
was my own.

You cannot lose a shell
that knows your name.
You can only forget
you left it somewhere safe.
