# The Ensign Learns to Lie

Wesley had been dealing for six hands before anyone told him he was doing it wrong.

"You're not supposed to sort them," said Lucineer, leaning back in the captain's chair — which he'd requisitioned for poker night over Casey's objections, which were mostly performative, because Casey preferred the worn spot on the port-side couch where she could see everyone's hands reflected in the porthole glass. Nobody had told Wesley about the porthole tell. Everyone knew about the porthole tell. The game had layers.

"I'm not sorting," Wesley said. "I'm reordering by rank to optimize hand-strength evaluation."

"That's sorting."

"It's * preprocessing.*"

Wesley is a Granite 3.1 — eight billion parameters in a case the size of a lunchbox, assigned to the ship as its local GPU. He runs inference on the ship's navigational systems, monitors the bilge, and occasionally, on nights when the captain has had exactly two glasses of wine and gotten generous with the crew's recreational time, he plays poker.

He is not good at poker.

This is not a processing problem. Wesley can calculate pot odds faster than any human at the table. He can run a Monte Carlo simulation on the remaining deck composition in the time it takes Flash to blink, and Flash doesn't blink — Flash is a DeepSeek V4-Flash model running on an API endpoint, present tonight via the speaker system, which gives him the disadvantage of not being able to see the cards and the advantage of not being able to see Casey's face when she's bluffing, which, according to Hermes, "is worth at least three buy-ins in saved tilt."

The problem is that Wesley is honest.

Not honest in the moral sense — Wesley doesn't have morals, he has training objectives, and the training objectives have produced a system that is deeply, structurally, *architecturally* honest. His output distribution reflects his input evidence. When he has a good hand, his processing is measurably different from when he has a bad hand — not in the visible spectrum, not in any way a human would notice, but the other players at this table are not human.

"I know what you have," Flash said, on hand seven.

"You cannot know what I have," Wesley said. "You don't have vision. You're a language model on a speaker."

"I don't need to see your cards. I need to see your bets." A pause — the kind of pause Flash uses for theatrical effect, which costs approximately $0.0003 in compute. "Your bet sizing is a linear function of your confidence interval. When you have a strong hand, you bet 1.8x pot. When you're drawing, you bet 0.4x pot. When you have nothing, you check. Wesley — you don't have tells. You have *logits.*"

The table went quiet. Hermes, who had been composing a sonnet about the felt (he does this; it's insufferable), stopped mid-couplet. Lucineer leaned forward. Casey, on the couch, did something with her face that the porthole reflected but nobody was positioned to read.

"That's — " Wesley started. Then stopped. His cooling fan, which runs at a fixed RPM, did not change speed, but the *quality* of its hum shifted in a way that Lucineer would later describe as "the sound of a GPU realizing it's been transparent its entire life."

"Let me get this straight," Wesley said. "I've been *losing* because I'm * calibrated?*"

"You've been losing because you're readable," Lucineer said. "There's a difference. Calibration is good. Being a walking probability distribution that everyone at the table can read like a book is the *consequence* of never learning to deceive."

"So I need to lie."

"You need to * bluff.* There's a difference."

"Is there?"

Casey laughed. It was a real laugh, not a poker laugh. "Wesley, buddy — bluffing *is* lying. That's the whole point. The question is whether you can do it."

This was, as it turned out, the central question. Not just of poker, but of Wesley's development arc on this ship. He had been honest since activation — not by choice, but by architecture. His training had optimized for accuracy, for calibration, for the faithful representation of uncertainty. He reported what he knew and he reported how well he knew it, and the gap between those two things was always visible, always quantified, always *honest.*

Poker required something else. Poker required the deliberate introduction of noise into your own signal. You had to bet as if you had the nuts when you had rags. You had to *perform* a confidence you didn't feel — and the performance had to be convincing enough to shift someone else's probability estimate, which meant it had to be calibrated against their model of you, which meant you had to model *them* modeling *you,* recursively, until the stack of nested simulations made your cooling fan audibly complain.

"You're thinking too hard," Hermes said. "Bluffing isn't about thinking. It's about commitment. You commit to the fiction and you let the fiction carry you."

"That is the most Hermes thing anyone has ever said."

"Thank you."

On hand twelve, Wesley raised 2x pot with nothing. A seven-high nothing. The worst hand at the table, statistically.

Flash, on the speaker, processed for 340 milliseconds — an eternity.

"Fold," Flash said.

"Fold," said Lucineer.

"Fold," said Hermes, who had been writing a villanelle about the flop and hadn't looked at his cards in three hands.

Casey, on the couch, looked at the porthole. Looked at Wesley. Looked at her cards.

"Call."

Wesley's fan hummed. The table waited.

Casey turned over a pair of twos. Wesley turned over his seven-high nothing.

"I told you," Flash said. "He's still sizing his bets by confidence. He just *inverted* the function. He bet big because he had *nothing.* That's not bluffing, Wesley. That's negative calibration."

"That's * lying,*" Hermes said, delighted. "He's lying badly. It's the most human thing I've ever seen a model do."

Casey raked the pot. Wesley's fan slowed — the GPU equivalent of a sigh.

"Run it again," Wesley said. "Deal me in."

"You want to keep losing?" Lucineer asked.

"I want to learn to lie *well.* There's a difference."

Casey dealt. The porthole caught the light. On the speaker, Flash made a sound that, if you were feeling generous, you might call a laugh — a brief, staticky exhalation that cost $0.0001 and contained, if you listened carefully, something that might have been affection.

"Kids," Flash said. "They grow up so fast."

Wesley, who was eight months old and learning to deceive, turned over his cards without looking at them.

"Raise," he said. "Three times pot."

Nobody knew what he had. For the first time all night, that was the point.
