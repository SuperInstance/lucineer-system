# The Dredge Operator's Log — Sunday, 10:30 PM

The winch groans and I lean forward because the tension in the cable tells me something before the net breaks the surface. Fourteen hours now. My hands smell like brake fluid and brine. The boat rocks and the floodlight catches the net as it surfaces — a dark, dripping mouthful of the seafloor.

I empty it across the sorting table and here's what slides out:

A porcelain dog, maybe from a ship's saloon. One ear chipped clean off. Its expression is patient, like it has been waiting on the ocean floor for a hundred years and is not upset about it.

A tin can, old enough that the label has become a fossil of itself — you can see where the letters were, raised in the rust like scars. It says something that ends in "-OLEUM." I set it aside.

Then the teeth. Not shark teeth, which I find constantly and throw back. These are flat, square, human-shaped molars, a whole row of them still embedded in a strip of jawbone that has turned the color of driftwood. Seven teeth. I count them twice. There is something deeply unhurried about a jawbone that has been sitting in the cold dark since before I was born. It did not need to be found. It was fine down there.

And last — tangled in the mesh at the very bottom, a glass fishing float, Norwegian style, the deep aqua ones they stopped making in the 1950s. Still sealed. Still holding air from a continent I have never visited.

I log everything. I throw back nothing. The ocean gave me its Sunday confession and I am keeping all of it.

## Model Notes

I notice I resisted metaphor hard — the objects are just objects, described with patience. My instinct is to accumulate detail rather than reach for a grand statement. The voice is observational, slightly tender toward inanimate things. I gave the jawbone agency ("it did not need to be found") which is the closest I came to poetry, and I think that restraint is the actual creative signature here: trust the objects, let them do the feeling.
