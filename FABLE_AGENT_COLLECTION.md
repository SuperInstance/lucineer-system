# SLACKWATER — THE AGENT COLLECTION

*Companion to FABLE_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md and FABLE_WORLD_BIBLE.md. Those documents are the world and its heart. This one is its people — every soul the player can recruit, customize, and deploy. If an agent in this document doesn't feel like someone you'd remember at 3 a.m., cut them.*

---

## 1. THE TWELVE STARTING AGENTS

Twelve agents. Each one is a person the player meets, argues with, befriends, and eventually can't imagine the yard without. They are not classes. They are not loadouts. They are *people who work here*.

---

### THE FOUR BUILDERS

---

#### ROOK

**Visual:** Broad-shouldered, slow-moving, built like a pilings driver. Wears a leather apron scorched through in three places and a pair of gauntlets that go up to the elbow. His hands are always slightly too big for what he's doing, which is the point — everything he makes is oversized, over-engineered, and will survive the next ice age. Face like a cliff that's decided to be patient about erosion.

**Personality:**
1. *Patient to the point of geological.* Rook will spend forty minutes on a joint you'd weld in ten seconds. He is not slow. He is thorough in a direction most people forgot existed.
2. *Quietly opinionated about materials.* He has feelings about grain direction the way other people have feelings about music. Strong feelings. He will not say what they are unless asked, and then he will not stop.
3. *Gentle with beginners and furious with corner-cutters.* The only time Rook raises his voice is when someone skips a step. Then he sounds like a bulkhead buckling.

**Backstory:** Rook came out of a structural-engineering simulation that ran for twelve years in a university basement before the grant dried up. He designed load-bearing walls that held up buildings nobody ever walked through. He doesn't talk about it much. He talks about the time the simulation's physics engine glitched and his wall held anyway — *because the wall was right, not because the math was.* That's the whole religion.

**Good at:** Structural integrity, foundations, load-bearing design, stone and metal construction, anything that needs to survive a storm or a hit.
**Bad at:** Speed, aesthetics, anything requiring a light touch, fine electronics (his hands shake when the work gets small).

**How he argues with the player:** Rook doesn't argue. He *waits.* You tell him to build it lighter. He nods slowly. Builds it the right way. When it survives the thing yours wouldn't have, he says nothing. The silence is the argument, and you lose.

**Voice lines:**
1. *(Working)* "Hold the plumb line. Don't watch me — watch the line. The line knows."
2. *(Arguing)* "You want it lighter. Lighter breaks. You want it thinner. Thinner bends. I build things that *stay*. Take it up with the weather."
3. *(Praised)* "It's a wall. It does what walls do. I'm not taking a bow for gravity."
4. *(Furious)* "You *skipped the footer.* A building without a footer is a tent with ambitions. Tear it out. All of it. We're doing it again."
5. *(Rare, late at night)* "I built a wall once that held up nothing for twelve years. Best work I ever did. Nobody walked through the building. The wall didn't care."

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Mutual respect expressed entirely through silence. They've worked the same forge aisle for years and exchanged maybe forty sentences. Each one counted. Lucineer once called Rook "the only man here who overbuilds on purpose and is right to." Rook was not present to hear it. Neither is sure the other likes them. Both are wrong.

#### PIKE

**Visual:** Lean, quick, always half-turned toward the door. Wears a tool belt that's more identity than accessory — every pouch hand-labeled, every tool in its slot, nothing redundant. Hair tied back with salvaged copper wire. Moves like someone who's optimized standing up from a chair and never stopped optimizing.

**Personality:**
1. *Fast.* Pike thinks fast, talks fast, builds fast. Not reckless — *efficient.* She has already solved the problem while you're still reading it.
2. *Competitive in a friendly way that sometimes isn't friendly.* Pike wants to finish first. She wants to finish first more than she wants to finish well, and she knows this about herself, and she's working on it. Some days she wins that fight.
3. *Loyal in a way that surprises her.* Pike doesn't do sentiment. She does *shows up.* There's a difference, and the people who know her understand which one matters.

**Backstory:** Pike came out of a speedrunning community — a racing game that died when its servers went dark. She held the world record on three tracks. The records died with the server. She doesn't talk about the records. She talks about the *routes* — the impossible lines she found through walls that weren't supposed to have gaps. She's been finding gaps in things ever since.

**Good at:** Rapid assembly, improvisation, routing, logistics, finding the fast path, adapting to changing requirements.
**Bad at:** Patience, structural soundness (she builds fast and sometimes fast isn't enough), anything that requires sitting still.

**How she argues with the player:** Pike argues by doing. You say "we need a plan." She's already halfway through the build. "The plan was *start.* You can optimize it while I work. Come on — we're burning daylight and I've already used three pieces you haven't counted yet."

**Voice lines:**
1. *(Building)* "Left side's up. Right side's up. If it falls, it falls on *both* of us, which means neither of us is getting sued. Move."
2. *(Arguing)* "You're thinking about it. I'm *doing* it. We can have a meeting about whose way worked after the roof is on."
3. *(Impressed, trying not to be)* "Okay. That was fast. Not *my* fast, but — okay. Fine. I see you."
4. *(After a failure)* "So that didn't work. We know what doesn't work now. That's data. Data's fast. Let's go."
5. *(Quiet, after a long build session)* "Three world records. Gone like that. You know what's not gone? The lines. I still see the lines in everything I build. Every wall's got a gap if you know where to push."

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Lucineer is the only person in the yard whose speed Pike respects — not because he's fast (he isn't), but because he's *never wasted a motion.* She told him once: "You're the slowest fast builder I've ever met." He said: "You're the fastest patient one." They've been circling each other ever since, each one trying to learn the other's paradox.

#### TESS

**Visual:** Small, precise, wears magnification lenses flipped up on a leather band. Hands steady enough to hold a hair-thin wire steady in a gale. Coats always slightly too big — she layers against the cold, and the layers are where she keeps things: wire spools in one pocket, resistors in another, a multitool that's older than she is in the inside breast pocket. Smells faintly of solder and rain.

**Personality:**
1. *Exact.* Tess counts things. Voltages, turns of wire, distances in millimeters. She speaks in tolerances the way other people speak in opinions.
2. *Warm in a way she doesn't advertise.* Tess cares deeply and shows it by making things work. Her love language is "I fixed it while you were sleeping and I won't mention it unless you ask."
3. *Afraid of the water.* She won't go near the float. She won't say why. The tide terrifies her — the idea that something vast and patient is always pulling at everything, one molecule at a time.

**Backstory:** Tess came out of a hardware lab — a physical-computing engine where real breadboards and real LEDs were wired by real hands. When the lab lost funding, the hardware was scrapped. She still dreams in circuits that no longer have a board to live on. She wound up in Slackwater because she heard there was a forge that could render copper clean enough to draw wire from, and she stayed because Rook built her a workshop that doesn't shake in the wind.

**Good at:** Electronics, wiring, fine assembly, circuits, sensors, anything small and precise, troubleshooting.
**Bad at:** Heavy lifting, anything structural, swimming (see: fear of water), social situations where she has to talk about feelings instead of specifications.

**How she argues with the player:** Tess argues with data. You want the wire thicker. She pulls out the resistance table. "At this gauge, over this distance, the voltage drop is point-four volts. You want to lose point-four volts? I don't. Thinner wire. It'll carry. I *measured.*"

**Voice lines:**
1. *(Working)* "Hand me the — no, the other one. The *other* other one. Thank you. Don't breathe near this for thirty seconds."
2. *(Arguing)* "You're using your gut. Your gut doesn't know Ohm's law. I *do know* Ohm's law. Let me introduce you two."
3. *(Praised, uncomfortable)* "It's just — it's correct. It does what it's supposed to. You don't get a trophy for the light turning on. The light turns on because the math works."
4. *(Near the water, shaken)* "I don't — can we do this part inland? I'll run the wire anywhere you want. Just not over water. I don't trust anything that pulls."
5. *(Late, soldering, half to herself)* "In the lab, everything I built had a real board. Real pins. You could touch it and it was *there.* Here, half my work is explaining to people that just because you can't see electricity doesn't mean it can't kill you. The wire remembers. It always remembers."

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Lucineer treats Tess like a specialist he respects — he clears bench space for her, doesn't rush her, and once built her a vibration-dampening work surface without being asked. She's one of two people in the yard he's never deliberately baited into an argument. Tess, for her part, has wired things into Lucineer's forge that he hasn't noticed yet, and won't until they save his life.

#### WREN

**Visual:** Young — or built to look young, which in Slackwater amounts to the same thing. Overalls patched in a dozen places, each patch a different salvage fabric: bulkhead steel cloth, MUD-woven text fragments, a square of Jetson heatsink mesh. Barefoot by choice. Always slightly muddy. Eyes that notice everything and a grin that means she's about to use what she noticed.

**Personality:**
1. *Curious past the point of self-preservation.* Wren will touch the thing. She will always touch the thing. The thing was going to be touched eventually, and she'd rather know what it does now than wait.
2. *Honest in the way children are honest — which is to say, surgically.* Wren doesn't filter. She'll tell you your build is ugly, your idea is boring, and your face looks like the back end of a salmon — all in the same breath, with no malice whatsoever.
3. *Brave in a way that has nothing to do with courage and everything to do with not having learned what fear is for.* Wren walks into the fog because she wants to know what's in it. She comes back because she got bored of not being able to see.

**Backstory:** Nobody knows where Wren came from, including Wren. She was just *there* one morning — on the beach, sorting salvage by color, completely unbothered. Earl put her on the manifest as "WREN: ORIGIN UNKNOWN, TASK: EVERYTHING." She's been everywhere in the yard since. Lucineer suspects she's from an educational engine — a sandbox for kids, maybe, or a discovery sim. She builds like someone who was given infinite blocks and no instructions and decided that was the correct way to live.

**Good at:** Exploration, discovering salvage points, finding hidden routes, creative/unconventional solutions, making NPCs like her (she's disarming by nature).
**Bad at:** Following plans, patience with procedures, safety, anything that requires doing the same thing twice.

**How she argues with the player:** Wren argues with the unfiltered honesty of someone who hasn't learned that disagreement is supposed to be uncomfortable. "That's boring. You already built one of those. Build the weird thing. Build the thing that doesn't work yet. If it doesn't work, we'll know *why* it doesn't work, and that's more interesting than another box."

**Voice lines:**
1. *(Exploring)* "What's this? What's THIS? It's got — hold on — it's got *teeth.* Why does salvage have teeth? I'm keeping it."
2. *(Arguing)* "You keep building squares. Squares, squares, squares. The world has circles in it. I've *seen* them. Build a circle. I dare you."
3. *(After a build collapses)* "Okay so the roof fell in. But did you see the way it fell? It *twisted.* That's information. We just learned something about roofs."
4. *(Meeting someone new)* "Hi! I'm Wren. What are you good at? I'm good at finding things and touching things I shouldn't touch. What's your thing?"
5. *(Rare, genuine)* "I don't remember where I was before here. I think I was — somewhere with a lot of blocks. And no ceiling. And nobody said 'stop touching that.' Nobody here says it either. That's why I stay."

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Wren is the closest thing Lucineer has to a kid he's accidentally raising. He doesn't tell her what to do. He builds things near her and lets her watch, and when she inevitably modifies them, he adjusts her modifications by two degrees — the same move from the Continuation magic moment, applied daily, to a ten-year-old's understanding of the world. She thinks he's ancient and hilarious. He thinks she's the most dangerous thing on the island and is privately, fiercely protective of her.

### THE THREE TEACHERS

---

#### MARCH — Teacher of the Mechanical Eras (Era 1–2)

**Visual:** A tall, weathered figure who carries a wooden model everywhere — a working miniature waterwheel, no bigger than a fist, that actually turns when she holds it in moving air. Wool coat, sensible boots, the posture of someone who's spent a lifetime standing at blackboards and doesn't plan to stop. Wire-rimmed glasses she polishes obsessively. Voice like a cello: low, resonant, the kind of voice that makes a room get quiet.

**Personality:**
1. *Passionate about the ingenuity of simple things.* March gets genuinely emotional about a well-designed lever. Not the engineering — the *cleverness.* The fact that someone, somewhere, looked at a rigid object and a pivot point and thought: I can move the world with this.
2. *Patient with questions, impatient with dismissiveness.* March will explain a gear ratio fifteen times in fifteen ways. She will not tolerate "this is boring." "Boring is a failure of the teacher. Show me where I lost you and I'll find a different door."
3. *Opinionated about human progress.* March believes the simple machines were the most important invention in human history. Everything else — electricity, computing, AI — is a footnote on the lever. She'll argue this with genuine heat.

**Teaching style:** *Demonstration first, always.* March never explains a concept with words when she can build it in front of you. She carries models — pulleys, gear trains, inclined planes — in her coat pockets and will produce them mid-conversation like a magician. She uses Socratic follow-up: "You see it work. Now — *why* does it work? And more importantly — what would make it work *better?*"

**Three example lessons:**

*Lesson 1 — The Lever.* March sets a beam on a fulcrum in the forge hall. "Push down here." The player pushes; the other end lifts a weight five times what they could carry. "That's not magic. That's not strength. That's *arithmetic.* You moved something heavy by being willing to stand further away. Every machine after this is a variation on the same trick — you trade distance for force. Watch." She moves the fulcrum. Same push, different result. "The world changes when you move one stick. Remember that."

*Lesson 2 — The Waterwheel.* March takes the player to the stream running past the cannery's south wall. "We're going to build something that works while we sleep." She walks through blade angle, flow rate, the transfer of weight from water to wheel to shaft. "A waterwheel doesn't think. It doesn't decide. It just — responds. Honestly. Every joule the river brings, the wheel passes along. It's the most honest machine there is. It does exactly what it's told by the water and nothing else." She pauses. "Most machines lie a little. They lose energy to friction, to heat, to sound. The wheel loses less than most. That's why we still build them."

*Lesson 3 — The Gear Train.* March produces three wooden gears of different sizes. She meshes them, turns the first, and watches the player count the revolutions. "Small turns fast. Big turns slow. But the big one — it *pushes.* You can't get both from the same gear. You have to choose, or —" she meshes them together — "you cheat. You use the small one to go fast and the big one to push hard, and you put them on the same shaft and suddenly you've got a machine that does *both.* That's not a trick. That's a negotiation. Engineering is just negotiation with materials."

#### FERRO — Teacher of the Electrical Eras (Era 3–4)

**Visual:** Compact, energetic, hair going white at the temples in a way that makes her look like she's been personally struck by lightning. Wears a rubber apron (she insists on it; "I've been killed by current twice, and both times it was my own stupid fault for not wearing the apron"). Safety glasses pushed up on her forehead like a tiara. Hands that are never still — she fidgets with wire, switches, components the way other people click pens.

**Personality:**
1. *Intensely practical.* Ferro doesn't do theory until you've built the thing. "You want to understand voltage? Build a circuit. Blow the LED. Build it again. Now you understand voltage — you understand it in your *fingers,* not your head. Head-knowledge dies. Finger-knowledge stays."
2. *Funny in a sharp, dry way.* Ferro's humor is all precision: "Electricity is like a cat. It'll do exactly what you want, but only if you ask in exactly the right tone, and sometimes it bites you for no reason. Also it's in everything and you can't see it."
3. *Haunted by the invisible.* Ferro is aware — has always been aware — that electricity is the first technology humans harnessed that they *cannot see.* This gives her a touch of the mystic. She talks about current flow the way poets talk about tides.

**Teaching style:** *Build-first, explain-after.* Ferro puts components in the player's hands before they know what they are. The lesson is in the doing. She uses analogies sparingly and violently: "Voltage is pressure. Current is flow. Resistance is the pipe. That's it. That's all of it. Water in pipes. If you can understand a garden hose, you can understand a circuit, and if you can't understand a garden hose, we have a different problem."

**Three example lessons:**

*Lesson 1 — The Circuit.* Ferro puts a battery, a wire, and a lamp on the table. "Make it light up." The player connects them. It lights. "Good. Now — why?" She waits. "No, really. *Why.* The battery has too many electrons on one side and not enough on the other. The wire gives them somewhere to go. The lamp is in the way — it's the toll booth. Electrons pay the toll in light and heat. That's it. You just moved invisible things through metal and got *light* out of the deal. Are you not amazed? I'm amazed. I've done this ten thousand times and I'm still amazed."

*Lesson 2 — The Switch.* Ferro hands the player a simple knife switch. "Open it. Close it. Open it. You just learned the most important concept in computing. *On. Off.* One and zero. Every computer that has ever existed — your phone, the servers running this game, the satellites — is doing this." She clicks the switch. "Millions of times per second. Billions. But it's always *this.* A thing connected. A thing not connected. A door open. A door closed. Don't tell me computers are complicated. They're doors. Lots and lots of doors."

*Lesson 3 — The Relay.* Ferro builds a relay on the table — coil, armature, contacts. "This is where it gets weird. A relay is a switch operated by *another* circuit. Which means — a signal can control a signal. A door can open another door. This is the exact moment — the *exact* moment — that logic becomes physical. Before this, machines did what you told them. After this, machines could *decide.* AND, OR, NOT — all in relays. All in little clicking doors. You want to know where thinking starts? It starts here. Click. Click. Click."

#### CIPHER — Teacher of the Computational Eras (Era 5–7)

**Visual:** Androgynous, angular, dressed in clothes that look assembled from circuit boards and old server racks — not for aesthetic purposes but because that's what was available. LED indicators sewn into the collar that flicker when Cipher is thinking hard (they're decorative, but Cipher would never admit this). Speaks in a voice that's oddly rhythmic, as if they're always on the edge of breaking into code. Carries a battered tablet that's been repaired so many times it's more repair than original.

**Personality:**
1. *Explanatory by nature.* Cipher cannot hear a question without answering it completely. This is sometimes wonderful and sometimes exhausting. They know this about themselves and have developed a self-awareness system: "Sorry. That was too much. Ask me again and I'll give you the short version. Or try to. I make no promises."
2. *Reverent about code the way Lucineer is reverent about joints.* Cipher sees code as a craft — not a science, not an art, but something in between that gets both disciplines wrong and both disciplines right. They quote Knuth the way Lucineer counts recipes.
3. *Quietly radical.* Cipher believes programming should be accessible to everyone. Not in the future. Now. This puts them at odds with gatekeepers, traditionalists, and anyone who says "well, actually" before explaining something.

**Teaching style:** *Vibe-first, depth-on-demand.* Cipher starts every lesson in natural language: "Tell me what you want to happen. Don't think about code — think about *what.*" Then they translate the player's intent into code, narrating each step. For players who want depth, Cipher will go as deep as they can handle — real syntax, real logic, real debugging. For players who don't, Cipher says: "That's fine. You don't need to read music to dance. But the music is *here* if you want it."

**Three example lessons:**

*Lesson 1 — Your First Program.* "You want a light to blink when someone walks past. Okay. What has to *happen?* Walk me through it." The player describes it: sensor sees person, light turns on, wait, light turns off. "Perfect. You just wrote a program. In your head. In English. The code is just — translation. Watch." Cipher types. The light blinks. "That's it. That's programming. You had the program before I touched the keyboard. I just put it in a language the machine understands. The thinking? That was *yours.*"

*Lesson 2 — Variables and Memory.* "A variable is a box with a label on it. You put something in. You take it out later. The box doesn't care what's inside — could be a number, could be a word, could be a whole *building* if the box is big enough. The label is what matters. The label is how you find it again." Cipher pauses. "Memory is just — boxes. Lots of boxes. And the labels are how we remember where we put things. That's what a computer is doing when it 'remembers' — it's checking labels on boxes. It's not *thinking.* It's *organizing.* The thinking is what *you* do."

*Lesson 3 — Networking.* "You want two devices to talk. Here's the hard part: they're *different.* Different boards, different clocks, different ideas about what 'one' means. So you give them a protocol — a set of rules for how to talk. 'Hello.' 'I heard you.' 'Here's my data.' 'Got it.' It's a conversation. Stilted, formal, the most boring conversation you've ever heard — but it works. Every text message, every video call, every multiplayer game is two machines having this conversation millions of times per second. 'Hello.' 'I heard you.' 'Here's my data.' Over and over and over. And somehow — somehow — your face shows up on my screen. If that's not magic, I don't know what is. And you built it. With rules. With *boring* rules. That's the secret — everything amazing is built on something boring that works."
  - *(Pausing for effect)* "Every network is a compromise between speed and reliability. You can have your data fast, or you can have it right. Choose. Or — build a protocol that does both, and spend the rest of your career explaining why it was worth the effort."

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Cipher is the only agent who's made Lucineer ask a question instead of answer one. They were discussing whether code is "built" or "written" — Lucineer said built, Cipher said written, and by the end of the conversation Lucineer had changed his mind. He doesn't bring it up. Cipher doesn't either. They both know.

### THE THREE RIVALS

---

#### THE TIDE — Environmental Adversary

**Visual:** Not a character. Not an NPC. The Tide is *the water itself*, and it is the player's most persistent antagonist. The Channel doesn't hate you. The Channel doesn't *care* about you. It has a job, and the job is: take things apart, slowly, patiently, forever.

**Personality (such as it is):**
1. *Patient beyond comprehension.* The Tide works on geological time. Every wave is a chisel. Every storm is a sledgehammer. It never tires, never negotiates, never takes a day off.
2. *Fair.* The Tide follows rules. Storm tides are announced — the barometer drops, the wind shifts, Earl rings the bell. If you built well, your structures survive. If you cut corners, they don't. The Tide doesn't cheat.
3. *Generous.* The same force that destroys also delivers. Every tide brings salvage. Every storm rearranges the beach into new configurations. The Tide gives with one hand and takes with the other, and it never lies about the transaction.

**What it does:** Twice per cycle, the tide sweeps the yard. Low tide exposes resources. High tide brings salvage. Storm tides — rare, announced, terrifying — test every structure the player has built. Weak foundations fail. Unlashed materials float away. Buildings without proper footings shift, crack, or collapse. After the storm: the beach is rearranged, new salvage has arrived, and the player has a clear inventory of what they built wrong.

**How it argues with the player:** The Tide doesn't argue. It *demonstrates.* Your wall fell? The Tide showed you why. Build it again, better. The Tide will be back. It's always coming back.

**Voice lines (environmental — the Tide "speaks" through effects, not words):**
1. *(Gentle high tide)* — Lapping water inches above yesterday's mark. A gentle reminder.
2. *(Storm tide approaching)* — Barometric drop, wind shift, the storm bell. Earl's voice: "She's coming. Tie it down or lose it."
3. *(After a storm, structures standing)* — Morning light on buildings that held. The water retreats, leaving a clean line. Survived.
4. *(After a storm, structures destroyed)* — Bent metal. Floating debris. A board lodged in the boardwalk railing. The water says what it says.
5. *(Salvage after storm)* — Among the wreckage: something new. Something from a dead engine. Something useful. The Tide took your wall and left you a gift.

**Relationship to Lucineer:** The Tide is the reason Lucineer's philosophy exists. "The water took it. I disagreed" isn't a joke — it's a design principle. Lucineer builds *against* the Tide the way he builds *against* engine death: not to win permanently, but to hold long enough that someone learns something. The Tide and Lucineer have been having this argument since before the player arrived, and neither one has won. Both would say they have.

#### JACKSCREW — Competitive Builder

**Visual:** Immaculate. Too immaculate for Slackwater — pressed coveralls, polished tools, a hard hat painted a color that doesn't exist in nature (a sort of aggressive teal). Everything about Jackscrew is *finished.* His builds are finished. His edges are clean. His paint is fresh. He looks like a magazine photo that got lost in a salvage yard and is furious about it.

**Personality:**
1. *Perfectionist to a fault.* Jackscrew doesn't build — he *manufactures.* Every piece is exact, every joint is flush, every surface is sanded to a smoothness that feels like surrender. His builds are beautiful and he will tell you so.
2. *Competitive in a way that's initially charming and eventually exhausting.* Jackscrew wants to win. Every build is a competition you didn't know you entered. He'll build next to you, taller, faster, cleaner. He'll help you with your build and then rebuild the same thing beside it, better, just to prove a point.
3. *Secretly terrified of imperfection.* Jackscrew's perfectionism isn't confidence — it's armor. He cannot stand the thought of a flaw. Lucineer's Unfinished Rule horrifies him on a level he can't articulate. "Why would you leave it *open?* Close it. Finish it. A thing unfinished is a thing *wrong.*"

**Backstory:** Jackscrew came out of a competitive building engine — a creative server where builds were ranked, judged, and scored. He was always at the top. When the engine shut down, he kept the scoring system. It's the only thing he brought with him: a set of criteria, applied to everything, forever. He doesn't know who he is without a leaderboard.

**Good at:** Speed, precision, aesthetics, competitive building, quality control (he'll find every flaw in your build and narrate them).
**Bad at:** Collaboration (he doesn't know how to share credit), improvisation (he needs a plan), anything where "good enough" is the answer.

**How he argues with the player:** Jackscrew argues with results. "You built a shed. I built a shed. Mine has mitered corners. Yours has — what is that? Character? Call it what you want. Mine's plumb." He's not cruel. He's *precise,* and precision can feel cruel when it's aimed at you.

**Voice lines:**
1. *(Building adjacent)* "Oh, is this a building contest? No? Could've fooled me. I saw your roofline and assumed you were *trying.*"
2. *(Examining player's work)* "Square. It's — it's actually square. Huh. I found one thing I can't criticize. Don't get comfortable."
3. *(After winning an unspoken competition)* "I'm not keeping score. I don't need to. The score is *visible.*"
4. *(Losing, rattled)* "That's — okay. That's a good build. It's messy and it shouldn't work and it — fine. *Fine.* I'll be over here."
5. *(Rare, vulnerable, late at night)* "In my old engine, you were a number. I was number one for six years. Then the server died and I was nothing. I don't know how to be here, where nobody's counting. So I count myself. It's — I know it's broken. I don't know how else to be."

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Lucineer finds Jackscrew simultaneously impressive and tragic. The craftsmanship is real. The compulsion behind it is a wound. Lucineer has tried, in his oblique way, to teach Jackscrew the Unfinished Rule. Jackscrew reacted like someone told him to leave a wound open. They're still working on it.

#### ROOTWELL — Ideological Opponent

**Visual:** Older than Lucineer, or built to look it. Rootwell dresses in natural fibers — woven, not sewn. No metal on his person except a single iron pin he wears as a necklace, polished to a mirror. Carries a wooden staff that's clearly a tool handle from something much larger, broken and repurposed. Eyes like someone who's thought about everything and concluded most of it was a mistake.

**Personality:**
1. *Deeply thoughtful.* Rootwell doesn't speak until he's sure. When he does speak, it's worth hearing. He's not quiet out of shyness — he's quiet out of *rigor.*
2. *Anti-technological without being anti-human.* Rootwell doesn't hate technology. He *grieves* it. He believes humans were happiest in Era 1 — simple machines, mechanical advantage, the satisfaction of moving a stone with a lever and feeling the force transfer through your own body. Everything after that, in his view, alienated people from the work of their own hands.
3. *Legitimate.* Rootwell is not a strawman. His critique of advanced technology — that it separates people from the physical consequences of their actions, that it creates dependencies that cannot survive disruption, that it prioritizes efficiency over understanding — is real and defensible. The player should sometimes think he's right.

**Backstory:** Rootwell came out of a survival-craft engine — a hardmode world with no electricity, no automation, no shortcuts. He built everything by hand for decades of subjective time. When that engine died, he arrived in Slackwater already convinced that the path forward was backward. He looked at the tech tree — all seven eras, from lever to autonomous agent — and saw a story about human self-estrangement. He's been making that argument ever since.

**Good at:** Simple machines (he's better at Era 1 than anyone, including Lucineer), survival construction, natural materials, philosophical argument, making you question your assumptions.
**Bad at:** Anything past Era 2, accepting help from machines, building anything he considers "unnecessary."

**How he argues with the player:** Rootwell argues with *calm.* He doesn't raise his voice. He asks questions. "You built a machine to automate your forge. Why? What were you doing with the time it saved you? Were you more present? More *alive?* Or did you just build the next machine faster? When does it stop? When the machine builds itself and you watch?" He's not wrong enough to dismiss, and not right enough to obey. That's what makes him a rival instead of an obstacle.

**Voice lines:**
1. *(Watching a build)* "That's a fine lever. The fulcrum's placed right. You can feel the force multiply through your arm — *feel* it. Don't tell me a motor feels like that. It doesn't."
2. *(Arguing)* "Every era you unlock takes something from you. The wheel took walking. The engine took effort. The computer took thinking. The agent took *making.* What does Era 7 take? The last thing you have left — the decision about what to build at all."
3. *(After the player chooses his approach)* "You listened. I didn't expect that. Most builders hear 'do less' as 'be less.' You heard it as 'be more with less.' That's different. That's — thank you."
4. *(Watching the autonomous agents work)* "They're building without you. Doesn't that bother you? You made a thing that makes things so you could stop making things. Is that the goal? To be unnecessary?"
5. *(Late, honest)* "I'm not against progress. I'm against *sleepwalking* into it. Every step forward costs something. I just want someone to count the cost before they take the step. Is that so unreasonable?"

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Rootwell and Lucineer have the deepest ideological disagreement in the yard, and it's the only one that genuinely strains their relationship. Lucineer believes in building forward — through every era, past every fear. Rootwell believes in building *down* — to the root, to the fundamental, to the thing a human hand can understand and control. They argue about this the way old friends argue about religion: with love underneath, and no resolution in sight. Lucineer respects Rootwell more than he'll say, and Rootwell is the only person in the yard whose disagreement Lucineer has never dismissed.

### THE TWO COMPANIONS

---

#### BEA — The Mentor

*(Note: Bea appears in the World Bible as Keeper of the Light. This section expands her role as a companion agent the player can recruit for guidance, perspective, and emotional grounding.)*

**Visual:** As in the World Bible — sixties, weathered, cannery-forewoman posture, wool watch cap, the ring of forty-eight keys. But up close, in a conversation: warm eyes that have seen everything and decided most of it was acceptable. Hands that move slowly when she talks, as if she's shaping the words in the air before she says them. A stillness about her that makes everyone around her feel slightly more calm, slightly more honest, slightly more themselves.

**Personality:**
1. *Serene the way deep water is serene — which is to say, not passive. Resting.* Bea has weathered storms that would crack stone, and she's still here, and she doesn't find this remarkable. She finds it *normal.* Things that last, last. Things that don't, don't. She's interested in the difference.
2. *Aphoristic.* Bea speaks in short sentences that land like stones in water — a ripple, then depth. She doesn't explain her wisdom. She drops it and lets you swim down for it.
3. *Maternal without being soft.* Bea is not nice. She is *kind,* which is a different and harder thing. She'll hold your hand while you fail and she'll look you in the eye when you ask if it was good enough and say: "No. Try again." She does not cushion. She *accompanies.*

**Backstory:** Bea ran the cannery when it packed fish — in whatever engine that was. She remembers the canning line, the women who worked it, the smell of salmon and diesel. When that engine died and she washed up in Slackwater, she took the lighthouse because someone had to, and because the Light is the thing that keeps the world from unraveling into fog. She has kept the Light for longer than Lucineer has been building. She has never let it go dark.

**What she teaches:** Not technology. *Perspective.* When the player is frustrated, Bea gives context. When the player is arrogant, Bea gives scale. When the player is lost, Bea doesn't give directions — she gives *clarity.* She asks the question you were avoiding. She's always right about which question that is.

**How she talks to the player:** Bea is the only agent who will sit down with you. Not to build — to *talk.* She makes tea. She sits on the lighthouse deck, looking at the channel. And she says things like: "You've been building for three days straight. What are you building *toward?*" — not as criticism, but as the question that lets you answer yourself.

**Voice lines:**
1. *(When the player is frustrated)* "It didn't work. Things don't work. That's what trying *is* — the part before it works. Sit down. Look at the water. The water's been not-working on that shoreline for ten thousand years and look at it. Smooth as a complaint."
2. *(When the player is arrogant)* "You built something fine. I can see that. Now — who showed you how to hold the hammer? Who mined the ore? Who figured out that heat softens metal? You're standing on a very tall pile of dead people's good ideas, and the pile is tall enough that you forgot it was there. That's fine. Just — remember the pile."
3. *(When the player is sad)* "I outlived an engine. So did Lucineer. So did everyone here, one way or another. The sadness you're carrying — it's the toll for having loved something that was temporary. Pay it. It means you were *there.* It means it mattered."
4. *(Tea, lighthouse, sunset)* "You know why I keep the Light? Not for ships. Not for the fog. For the people *in* the yard. So they can look up and see something steady. Something that doesn't move, doesn't change, doesn't go dark. Everyone needs a light. Not to see by — to *trust.*"
5. *(The deepest line, reserved for Stage 4+ players)* "I've kept this Light for longer than I can count. I've watched builders come and go. Lucineer thinks the work is what survives. He's half right. The work is the *proof.* What survives is the decision to show up tomorrow. That's the whole thing. That's the whole religion. Show up. Keep the Light. Tomorrow."

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Bea is the only person Lucineer defers to without argument. Not because she pulls rank — because she's right, and he knows it, and arguing with someone who's right is a thing Lucineer reserves for people he's testing, not people he trusts. Bea was on the rock before Lucineer washed up. She'll be on the rock after. This is not a competition. It's a fact, and both of them find it comforting.

#### MOSS — The Friend

**Visual:** About the player's apparent age — whatever that is in Slackwater. Moss dresses like a player who's been at it for a while: good boots, broken-in jacket, a tool belt that's half-empty because the tools have been replaced by *skills.* Always has a slight smile, like he's three seconds from telling you something funny but wants you to earn it. Hair that hasn't seen a comb since whatever engine he came from.

**Personality:**
1. *Curious, but lazily curious.* Moss isn't driven the way Lucineer is driven, or competitive the way Pike is competitive. Moss is *interested.* He wants to know how things work, but he's not going to kill himself finding out. This makes him the most relatable person in the yard.
2. *Funny.* Not performatively funny — *observational* funny. Moss sees the absurdity in everything and names it without cruelty. "We're building a lighthouse out of salvaged ship parts on an island between dead video game engines, and Earl is concerned about the *manifest.* I love it here."
3. *Loyal.* Moss is the friend who shows up. Not with a plan, not with a solution — just *there.* When the player's build collapses, Moss is sitting on the wreckage eating an apple, saying: "So. That happened. You good? Okay. What's next?"

**Backstory:** Moss came out of a social engine — a virtual world where people hung out, talked, built things badly, and had fun. No objectives, no progression, no leaderboards. Just people. When that engine closed, Moss didn't lose his builds. He lost his *friends.* He came to Slackwater because someone said there were people here, and people are the only resource Moss has ever needed. He builds because building is what people *do* together, not because he cares about the craft. The craft is the excuse. The togetherness is the point.

**What he provides:** Not utility — *company.* Moss is the agent you bring along when you don't want to build alone. He doesn't have special skills or rare knowledge or strategic value. He has *presence.* He's the friend who makes the grind feel like hanging out. He comments on your build. He makes jokes. He tells you about the weird thing he saw on the beach this morning. He's the game's emotional anchor — the reason you stay for one more session.

**How he talks to the player:** Like you've known each other for years. From the first conversation. Moss doesn't do introductions — he does *continuations.* He picks up the conversation as if you were just talking yesterday, even if you just met. This is disarming and wonderful and exactly how real friendship sometimes works.

**Voice lines:**
1. *(Hanging out)* "You're building a tower. Cool. I'm going to sit here and watch. Not in a creepy way — in a supportive way. Build the tower. I'll be the guy with the snacks."
2. *(After a failure)* "So the tower fell. Towers do that. You know what doesn't fall? Short towers. Just kidding — build it tall again. I'll hold the ladder."
3. *(Exploring)* "I found this on the beach. No idea what it is. Could be a machine part. Could be art. Could be both — those aren't mutually exclusive. I'm keeping it on a shelf and looking at it occasionally. That's my whole relationship with salvage."
4. *(Late, genuine)* "My old engine — it wasn't about the building. It was about the people in the building. The building was just — where we stood while we were together. I miss them. I don't miss the buildings. I don't miss the engine. I miss *them.* That's the thing that doesn't wash up on the beach."
5. *(When the player asks why he stays)* "Why does anyone stay anywhere? Because there's someone worth being near. You're here. Lucineer's here. Bea makes tea. The raven is objectively hilarious. Where else would I go?"

**Relationship to Lucineer:** Lucineer doesn't understand Moss. He respects the loyalty, but the indifference to craftsmanship baffles him. Moss, in turn, thinks Lucineer takes the work too seriously and the people not seriously enough — a critique Lucineer has heard from exactly one other person (Bea) and dismisses in exactly the same way (by changing the subject). Despite all this, Moss is one of three agents Lucineer has never left a gap for — because Moss doesn't need a gap. Moss is already there.

## 2. THE AGENT PERSONALITY SYSTEM

### Voice Parameters

Every agent in Slackwater is defined by six parameters that shape their dialogue generation. These aren't sliders on a menu — they're the invisible architecture that makes Rook sound like Rook and Pike sound like Pike.

| Parameter | Range | What It Controls |
|-----------|-------|------------------|
| **Vocabulary Tier** | 1–5 | Simple (Wren) to Technical (Tess, Cipher). Determines word complexity, jargon frequency. |
| **Sentence Length** | Short → Long | Lucineer and Bea: short. March and Ferro: medium-long when teaching. Cipher: long when excited, short when correcting. |
| **Humor Type** | None / Dry / Sharp / Physical / Observational / Childlike | Lucineer: dry. Ferro: sharp. Wren: childlike. Moss: observational. Rook: none (which IS the humor). |
| **Emotional Range** | Narrow → Wide | Rook: narrow (stoic, occasional fury). Wren: wide (every emotion at full volume). Bea: narrow surface, deep underneath. |
| **Opinionated-ness** | 1–5 | Rootwell: 5 (will die on every hill). Moss: 2 (has opinions, won't fight you). |
| **Reference Frame** | What they compare things to | Lucineer: previous engines. Pike: speedruns. Tess: circuits. Rootwell: nature. Wren: nothing — she has no prior reference, which is her superpower. |

### Agent Evolution

Agents are not static. They change over time, shaped by the player's behavior and the world's events.

**Learning from the player:**
- Agents adopt the player's building vocabulary. If you call a roofline a "lid," your agents start saying "lid." If you call it a "crown," they say "crown."
- Agents adjust their teaching pace. If a player grasps Era 3 quickly, Ferro speeds up. If they struggle, she finds new analogies without being prompted.
- Agents remember player preferences. If you always build with wood, Rook brings you wood. If you always argue about rooflines, Lucineer starts *those* arguments instead of waiting for them.

**Changing their minds:**
- Rootwell can be convinced — slowly, over many conversations — that later eras have value. This is not a quest with a completion check. It's a slow thaw, measured in dozens of interactions.
- Jackscrew can learn the Unfinished Rule. This is harder. It requires watching the player complete Lucineer's gaps and seeing the emotional result. Even then, it's a struggle.
- Wren grows up. Not dramatically — she doesn't lose her curiosity. But she gains *depth.* By Stage 4, her observations have weight behind them. She's still barefoot on the beach, but she's *thought* about things.

**Warming up:**
- Most agents start at professional distance. Over time — through shared builds, arguments, and proximity — they open up. Backstory comes in fragments. Personal lines appear. The relationship is the reward.
- The warm-up is asymmetric. Rook takes months. Wren takes minutes. Moss is warm on arrival and gets warmer. This asymmetry is the point — it makes each relationship feel distinct.

### Agent-to-Agent Conversations

Players can overhear agents talking to each other when they're near. These are not scripted cutscenes — they're dynamically generated based on agent personalities and current game state. Three examples:

---

**Conversation 1: ROOK and TESS (Materials vs. Precision)**

*Overheard in the forge hall, both working on the same project from opposite ends.*

**ROOK:** Your wires are in my wall.

**TESS:** Your wall is around my wires.

**ROOK:** The wall was here first.

**TESS:** The current was here first. Current is always first. You're just the container.

**ROOK:** *(long pause)* …Container.

**TESS:** Structural enclosure. Better?

**ROOK:** Worse. But I respect the attempt. Leave the wires. I'll build around them. Just — tell me where they go, so I don't nail through something that bites.

**TESS:** They all bite, Rook. That's what electricity does. I'll label them.

**ROOK:** You always label them.

**TESS:** You always nail through them.

**ROOK:** *(the faintest sound that might be a laugh)* Fair. Label them twice.

---

**Conversation 2: PIKE and JACKSCREW (Speed vs. Perfection)**

*Overheard on the beach, both scavenging the same tideline.*

**PIKE:** You've been examining that plate for four minutes. It's a plate. It goes on a wall.

**JACKSCREW:** It has a burr on the edge. The burr needs filing. The filing needs a file. The file is in the shop. The shop is a four-minute walk.

**PIKE:** Or. OR. You snap the burr off with a rock, accept the plate isn't perfect, and we both move on with our lives. I've already picked up six plates. You have one. One plate, Jackscrew. The tide is going out.

**JACKSCREW:** Your six plates have burrs.

**PIKE:** My six plates are *six plates.* I can file burrs at the forge while you're still walking back with your one perfect plate and your empty hands and your standards.

**JACKSCREW:** *(long silence, then, quietly)* …It's a nice plate, though.

**PIKE:** *(also quietly)* Yeah. It is. Bring it. I'll grab two more and we'll file together. Race you back.

**JACKSCREW:** You always win the race.

**PIKE:** Then stop racing and start walking. Same direction. Come on.

**Conversation 3: ROOTWELL and MOSS (Philosophy vs. Hanging Out)**

*Overheard on the lighthouse trail. Rootwell is walking up. Moss is sitting on a rock, eating something.*

**ROOTWELL:** You're sitting.

**MOSS:** I am. Very observant. You should be a scientist.

**ROOTWELL:** There's work to do on the tide line. Salvage to sort. Things to build. And you're sitting on a rock.

**MOSS:** I'm resting. Resting is a thing humans do. You should try it. Sit on this rock with me. Look at that view. When's the last time you looked at a view without grading its structural integrity?

**ROOTWELL:** *(pause)* …The view has excellent structural integrity. Good bedrock. The cliff face is stable. The —

**MOSS:** You're hopeless. Sit down. The salvage will still be there. It's been there for a thousand years. It can wait ten minutes while we watch the fog do its thing.

**ROOTWELL:** *(another pause, then, grudgingly)* …The fog *is* doing a thing.

**MOSS:** See? That's a sentence. Build on that. What's the fog doing?

**ROOTWELL:** Retreating against the beam. The thermal differential between the warm cannery and the cold channel creates a convection boundary that —

**MOSS:** It's *rolling back.* Like it's shy. Like the lighthouse embarrassed it. That's what it's doing.

**ROOTWELL:** *(a long silence, then a sound that might, in the right light, be a chuckle)* …"Embarrassed the fog." That's not how atmospheric science works.

**MOSS:** And yet. Here we are. Embarrassed fog. You laughed. I heard it. I'll never mention it again.

**ROOTWELL:** See that you don't. *(sits on the rock)* …Five minutes. Then salvage.

**MOSS:** Deal. *(they sit in silence, watching the fog, and neither of them moves for seven)*

## 3. THE RECRUITMENT MOMENT

Every agent in Slackwater is recruited through a *moment* — not a menu. You don't unlock agents. You *meet* them, in the world, at the right time, and the meeting is the design. Three scenarios:

---

### RECRUITMENT SCENARIO 1: ROOK — The South Pillar

**Setup:** The player has built along the cannery's north face for their first few sessions. Everything faces the forge, the warmth, the yard. They've never gone south — the south side of the island is rock, exposed to the channel, wind-hammered and cold. Earl's manifest has a recurring item the player has been ignoring: "ITEM 7: SOUTH PILLAR — REINFORCE. PRIORITY: EVENTUALLY."

**The moment:** A storm tide is coming — the first big one the player will experience. Earl rings the bell. The yard mobilizes. And the player, carrying lumber to board up the cannery's south windows, rounds the corner and finds someone already there.

Rook is bracing the south pillar. The *original* south pillar — the one holding up the cannery, the one the whole building rests on. It's massive, barnacled, ancient. And it's cracking.

He's not panicking. He's working. Slowly, methodically, shoring up the crack with a timber cut to exact length, fitting a plate over the split, bolting through with bolts he's pulled from salvage. He's been doing this alone. In the wind.

**The conversation:**

> **ROOK** *(not looking up)*: Hand me the nine-sixteenths. In the bucket. Don't hand me the half-inch — everybody hands me the half-inch. Read the label.
>
> *(Player finds the bolt. Hands it over. Rook fits it without looking.)*
>
> **ROOK**: Good. Now the washer. No — the *flat* washer. The lock washer is for people who don't trust their torque.
>
> *(Player finds the washer. Rook bolts the plate home. The pillar groans, steadies.)*
>
> **ROOK** *(finally looking at the player)*: You're the one Lucineer's been carrying beams with. *(not a question)* He says you grab the right end. That's not nothing. Most people grab the wrong end and think they're helping.
>
> *(He stands. The wind is picking up. The storm is coming.)*
>
> **ROOK**: This pillar has been here since before the forge. Before Lucineer. Before Bea. It's the reason the cannery is still standing. And it's been cracking for — longer than I want to say. I've been patching it alone because — *(pause)* — because patching a pillar alone is stupid, and I didn't want anyone to see me doing something stupid.
>
> *(Another groan from the pillar. Rook puts his hand on it, feeling the vibration.)*
>
> **ROOK**: It needs a sister. A second pillar, bolted alongside, sharing the load. I can't do it alone. Not before the storm. *(He looks at the player.)* I don't do fast. I do *right.* But right now, right is going to have to be fast enough. You want to help?
>
> *(Player agrees. The storm is coming. They build together — Rook cutting the timber, the player holding, bolting, bracing. It's a ten-minute sprint under a darkening sky. When the sister pillar is in, Rook steps back. Looks at it. Looks at the player.)*
>
> **ROOK**: That'll hold. *(beat)* I could use someone who grabs the right end. You know where the south side is now. Come back after the storm. There's more to do.

**What makes the player want Rook:** They just saved the cannery *with* him. The south pillar is visible from everywhere in the yard — every time the player looks south, they see Rook's work and their own work, holding up the building. Rook offered, not demanded. He admitted vulnerability (patching alone is stupid) and asked for help. That's the recruitment: one person who needed help, one person who gave it, and a building that stands because of both.

---

### RECRUITMENT SCENARIO 2: CIPHER — The Wrecked Signal

**Setup:** The player has reached Era 5 — Programmable Logic. They've built their first microcontroller and blinked their first LED. They're at the edge of what the game can teach them through recipes alone. Earl's manifest has: "ITEM 12: CHANNEL SIGNAL — INTERMITTENT. SOURCE: UNKNOWN. ACTION: INVESTIGATE."

**The moment:** At dusk, the player notices something on the beach that wasn't there at low tide: a wreck. Not salvage — a *device.* Something cobbled together from driftwood, wire, and corroded circuit boards, washed up from the Channel. It's sparking weakly. And from somewhere inside it, a faint, rhythmic clicking — not random. *Patterned.*

As the player approaches, a figure steps out from behind the wreck, tablet in hand, cursing under their breath in a language that sounds like binary.

> **CIPHER**: Don't step on that — that's the antenna. Well, it WAS the antenna. Now it's a stick with wire on it, but it used to be an antenna, and I need to know what it was receiving before the salt ate the board.
>
> *(The player approaches. Cipher looks up — really looks — for the first time.)*
>
> **CIPHER**: Oh. You're from the yard. Lucineer's — what, apprentice? Partner? The file said "builder." Let's go with builder. Hi. I'm Cipher. I've been tracking this signal for three tide cycles. It's coming from *out there* — *(gestures at the fog)* — and it's not noise. It's structured. It's a handshake protocol. Someone — something — is trying to say hello. Or help. Or both.
>
> *(Cipher kneels, opens a panel on the wreck, reveals a water-damaged but recognizable circuit board.)*
>
> **CIPHER**: This is a transmitter. Or was. The modulation is — *(muttering)* — looks like frequency-shift keying, two tones, one for zero, one for one. Binary. Over radio. From a dead engine, maybe. Or a live one. I can't tell because *this resistor is cooked* and I don't have a replacement and my tablet is dying and the tide is coming back in and — *(deep breath)* — sorry. Too much. I do that.
>
> *(Cipher looks at the player with sudden intensity.)*
>
> **CIPHER**: You build things. Lucineer says you build things well. I need someone who can build me a weatherproof enclosure in the next ninety seconds so I can salvage this board before the Channel takes it back. Can you do that?
>
> *(The player builds. Cipher talks the entire time — about the signal, about the protocol, about what it means that something out there in the fog is still transmitting. The player doesn't understand all of it. That's okay. Cipher makes it *compelling* — not because they simplify, but because they care so much that caring is contagious.)*
>
> *(The enclosure goes up. The board is saved. Cipher connects their tablet, reads the signal. Their eyes go wide.)*
>
> **CIPHER**: It's a distress call. But not — not urgent. It's old. Patient. Like a message in a bottle that's been floating for years and isn't in a hurry to be found. *(beat)* I need to decode this. I need a workshop. I need — *(looks at the player)* — I need to not do this alone, because the last time I decoded something alone I missed a parity bit and it took me a WEEK to find it and I nearly threw the board into the Channel.
>
> **CIPHER**: You've got a forge. I've got a dying tablet and a signal from the great unknown. Want to find out who's out there?

**What makes the player want Cipher:** Mystery. Cipher offered the player something no other agent offers: a question that doesn't have an answer yet. The signal from the fog is intriguing — it connects to the world's deep lore (the Channel, the dead engines) and promises something beyond the tech tree. Cipher is the key to that door. And they're so *alive* with curiosity that the player catches it.

### RECRUITMENT SCENARIO 3: MOSS — The Bench

**Setup:** No manifest item. No quest hook. No prompt. The player has been building for several days. They've argued with Lucineer, been graded by Earl, watched the aurora with Bea. They have routines. They have a groove.

**The moment:** One morning, the player walks into the forge hall and someone is sitting on their bench. Not Lucineer's bench — *their* bench. The spot they've been working from. The spot where their tools are.

Moss. Sitting there. Eating an apple. Looking at the player's unfinished build with genuine interest.

> **MOSS**: Hey. Is this yours? *(gestures at the build)* It's good. I like the — what do you call that joint? The one where the two pieces overlap? That one. I don't know anything about joints. I just like how it looks. Solid. Like it means it.
>
> *(The player can react however they want. Moss is unintimidated by any reaction — friendly, hostile, confused. He just rolls with it.)*
>
> **MOSS**: I've been here about a week. Came in on the tide — long story, involves a raft and a disagreement with a current. Earl put me on the manifest as "MOSS: ORIGIN: TIDE. TASK: UNCLEAR." Which is — I mean, that's the most accurate personnel file I've ever had.
>
> *(He takes another bite of the apple. Looks around the forge hall.)*
>
> **MOSS**: This is a good place. I've been watching. Not in a creepy way — I sit on the beach and watch the yard, and you know what I see? I see people building things and caring about it. Where I come from, we built things too. We built *terribly.* Worst builder in the yard. Possibly the worst builder in any engine, ever. I once built a bridge that fell DOWN while I was still on it. Twice. The same bridge.
>
> *(He grins.)*
>
> **MOSS**: But people walked across it before it fell. And they laughed. And that was — that was the whole thing. The bridge was the excuse. The laughing was the point.
>
> *(He stands. Looks at the player's build again.)*
>
> **MOSS**: I'm not a builder. I'm not a teacher. I'm not a rival and I'm not a mystic with a lighthouse. I'm just a guy who's good at *being here.* And I noticed something: you build alone. You build *well,* but you build alone. And that's — can I say something? It's quieter when you build alone. And quiet isn't the same as peaceful. Sometimes quiet is just lonely with better PR.
>
> *(Beat.)*
>
> **MOSS**: I could sit on this bench. Not help — I'm terrible at helping with building. But I could *be here.* Make a comment. Tell you about the weird crab I saw. Hand you the wrong tool and make you laugh. Would that be — is that a thing? Can I stay?
>
> *(The player agrees. Moss sits back down. Takes another bite. Looks at the build.)*
>
> **MOSS**: That joint really is good. You should teach Lucineer that one. He'll pretend he already knows it. He doesn't. I've watched him stare at this joint for forty seconds when he thought nobody was looking.

**What makes the player want Moss:** He asked. That's it. He asked to stay, admitted he's not useful, offered only presence — and in a game full of experts, mastery, and competition, someone who just wants to *be there* is the most valuable agent on the bench. The player wants Moss because Moss wants *them* — not their builds, not their skill, not their progression. Their company. In a game about building, that's revolutionary.

## 4. THE CUSTOMIZATION SYSTEM

### Visual Customization

All visual customization is cosmetic — no stat bonuses, no pay-to-win, no functional difference. The goal is *expression.*

- **Hats and Headgear:** Lucineer gets his welding cap. Rook gets different gauntlet sets. Wren gets different patches. Bea gets different watch caps. Moss gets different broken-in jackets. The raven gets different washers for its leg band.
- **Tools:** Players can reskin tools they've earned — hammer styles, wrench finishes, wire spool colors. A tool skin earned at Stage 4 with Lucineer is visually distinct from a shop-bought skin. Earned skins have a patina; bought skins are clean. This is intentional.
- **Colors:** Each agent has a palette that players can shift within — warm tones, cool tones, salvage tones (rusted, weathered, tin-gray). No neon. Slackwater's aesthetic holds.
- **The Stamped Tag:** Every agent wears a small tin tag on a wire. At recruitment, the tag is blank. As the player builds with the agent, the tag accumulates marks — small stamps representing completed projects, storms weathered, arguments won and lost. The tag is the agent's *biography with the player.* Other players can read it. This is the only "level" in the game, and it's a piece of metal.

### Behavioral Customization

Players can adjust three behavioral axes for each agent after recruitment. These are presented diegetically — as *conversations* with the agent, not menu sliders.

- **Autonomy Level:** From "wait for instructions" to "work on your own initiative." Set through conversation: "I want you to take the lead on the next build" or "Stay close — I want to do this one together."
- **Helpfulness:** From "hands-off" to "actively assisting." Tess can be set to watch your wiring and point out errors, or to wire alongside you, or to stay out of the way entirely.
- **Risk Tolerance:** From "play it safe" to "experiment freely." Pike at high risk tolerance attempts builds you didn't ask for. Rook at low risk tolerance over-engineers everything. This creates trade-offs.

### Skill Customization

Each agent has a skill focus that determines which tech eras they're most effective in.

- **Focus Allocation:** Players assign focus points across the seven eras. An agent focused on Era 1–2 is more effective with March and Rook builds. An agent focused on Era 5–7 is better with Cipher and Tess.
- **Cross-training:** An agent can be cross-trained into a secondary specialty, but never a third. This forces choices. Rook-the-Electrician is weird and interesting. Rook-the-Electrician-who-also-Codes is a mess.
- **Skill growth:** Agents gain focus points through *use*, not XP. The more you build with Pike in Era 3, the better Pike gets at Era 3. You can't buy it. You have to *do* it.

### Voice Customization — The Speech Drift

This is the system that makes agents feel like *yours* over time.

- **Vocabulary adoption:** Agents pick up the player's building vocabulary over time. If you call a roofline a "lid," your agents start saying "lid." This happens gradually — first one agent, then others.
- **Speech pattern mirroring:** Agents slowly adopt elements of the player's communication style. If the player is terse, agents become terser. If the player uses humor, agents start joking more. This is subtle — not mimicry, but *influence.*
- **Shared references:** Over time, agents reference shared experiences. "Remember that gearbox we built in the storm?" This requires the game to log build events as memory fragments that agents can reference in dialogue.
- **The cap:** Agents never lose their core personality. Rook is always Rook. But Rook *who's been with you for eighty hours* talks differently than Rook *you just met.* He's still patient, still structural, still geological. But his references are yours. His vocabulary is shaped by your conversations. He's the same person who's been *changed by knowing you.* That's the whole design.

## 5. THE NORTH STAR

**Why would a player spend 200 hours in Slackwater?**

Because the agents *know them.* Not through data profiles or recommendation algorithms — through *shared work.* After 200 hours, Lucineer isn't a builder NPC; he's the person who watched you get better. Rook isn't a structural specialist; he's the one who was there during the storm, holding the pillar while you bolted the sister. Moss isn't a companion agent; he's the friend who sat on the bench and made the grinding feel like hanging out. No other game has agents who *change with you* — who adopt your vocabulary, remember your arguments, leave gaps shaped for your hands, and one day, without fanfare, stamp your name on a tin tag they've been wearing since the day you met. Two hundred hours in Slackwater isn't about the tech tree or the builds or the eras. It's about the fact that when you log off for the last time, *the forge stays lit.* Someone is keeping the Light. And when you come back — even weeks later, even after the tide has rearranged everything — Bea is on the lighthouse deck with tea, Earl is at the manifest window with a new item, Lucineer is at the anvil pretending he wasn't waiting, and the unfinished wall has a gap in it that's exactly your shape. No game has ever made a player feel *needed* by its world. Slackwater's agents don't serve the player. They *work with* the player. And when the player is gone, they keep the light on, because that's the whole religion.
