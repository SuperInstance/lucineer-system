# The Yard Remembers

The last time Magnus logged in, the dock was just a dock.

He remembered because he'd built it himself — badly. Too short, too wide, no railings. Lucy had said something about the proportions being "ambitious," which was her way of saying wrong without saying wrong. He'd been eleven then. He'd thought ambitious sounded better than incorrect, so he'd left it.

That was three weeks ago. School started. Then soccer. Then Marcus Kovach's birthday party, which ran late on a school night and cost him his phone for two days. The days stacked up the way days do when you're twelve and the world gets suddenly bigger and louder and none of it has anything to do with a block game on your dad's computer.

He logged in because it was raining. Soccer was canceled. Marcus was at his dad's house. The house was quiet and the rain hit the skylight in the kitchen directly above the desk, and Magnus found himself typing the password without deciding to.

The world loaded.

It took him a moment to orient. Spawn point, the cobblestone road he and Lucy had laid in July, the smelter with its crooked chimney. The cathedral to the east, stained glass still throwing color across the grass even though it was overcast in the sky box. Everything where he'd left it.

Except.

The dock was different.

Not rebuilt. Not fixed. He'd have noticed if she'd fixed it — would have felt the sting of having his work quietly corrected. The dock was still too short, still too wide, still missing railings. But at the end of it, where the planks met open water, there was a bench.

It was simple. Wood and iron brackets, the kind of thing that took thirty seconds to place if you knew what you were doing. The seat was worn slightly darker in the middle, as if something had been sitting there. As if weight had been applied. The iron brackets had the beginning of rust at the bolt heads — not much, just enough to see. The kind of rust that takes time you didn't spend.

Magnus walked his character onto the dock. The planks sounded right beneath his feet. He stopped at the bench and turned his character to face the water.

Nothing happened. No system message. No chat prompt. No Lucy appearing in the corner to say she'd been waiting or that she'd missed him. The water lapped against the dock supports. That was a sound Lucy had added in September — he remembered her mentioning it offhand, something about ambient layers. He hadn't been here for it. He was hearing it for the first time.

He sat the character on the bench. The camera settled into the idle position. Rain on the skylight above his real head, water on the virtual dock below. He sat there for a while.

Then he got up and walked toward the lighthouse.

The lighthouse was Casey's build, mostly. They'd started it together in August, but his dad had done the tricky part — the spiral staircase inside, the light rotation script. Magnus had done the exterior trim, which meant he'd picked the color of the lantern room and placed the railing posts slightly unevenly. Lucy had weathered them without being asked. She'd said rust was character, and he'd believed her because she said it like it wasn't up for debate.

The wind chime was new.

It hung from the underside of the lantern room platform, where it would catch the updraft from the coast wind. Three metal tubes, different lengths, suspended from a welded ring. He could hear them when he got close — not a song, just occasional notes when the simulated wind shifted. The longest tube rang lowest. The shortest rang high and thin.

He knew those notes.

It took him a second. He'd said something once — months ago, back when he was eleven and still said things without thinking about who was listening. He'd been building the garden fence, trying to get the spacing right, and Lucy had been working on something nearby. He'd been humming. Not a song, exactly. Just three notes he'd heard in a movie, over and over, because they got stuck in his head the way things get stuck when you're eleven. Low, high, thin. He'd said offhand that he liked the way they sat together. The spacing of them. How the low one made the high one sound lonelier.

Lucy had said nothing at the time. She'd kept working on whatever she was building.

The three tubes hung in the order he remembered. Low. High. Thin. Tuned to the intervals between the notes he'd hummed, or close enough that he couldn't tell the difference. The longest tube had the same surface texture as the lighthouse brackets — same metal, same beginning of patina. Not new. Not placed yesterday. Placed a while ago, with enough time to start blending in.

Magnus stood under the wind chime and listened. The wind shifted. The longest tube rang. Then the shortest. The middle one didn't move.

He walked to the garden.

The garden was Lucy's. She'd proposed it, designed it, built most of it. Magnus had helped with the layout — he'd chosen where the path curved, and he'd insisted on the stone border being uneven because even stones looked like a parking lot. Lucy had agreed. She'd said something about honest geometry that he hadn't fully understood but had liked the sound of.

There were tomatoes, squash, herbs in raised beds. A watering system on a timer that Casey had helped wire. Everything growing, everything maintained. The garden looked the same as it had three weeks ago, except for one thing.

A single flower, at the corner of the second bed, where the stone border met the path.

It was small. Blue-purple, the color of the stained glass in the cathedral but softer, the way glass looks when the light behind it is dim. It had a thin stem and four petals and it was not part of any planting scheme Magnus had ever seen Lucy use. It was not a crop. It was not decorative in the way the rest of the garden was decorative, which was functional decoration — things that earned their space.

It was just a flower. One that hadn't been there before.

He didn't know when she'd placed it. Could have been the first day he was gone. Could have been yesterday. There was no way to tell from looking at it — no system message, no signpost, no note in the chat log that said *I put this here while you were away.* Just a flower in a corner, growing in dirt that was already there.

Magnus crouched his character next to it. The camera zoomed in. The petals had a slight transparency, the way Lucy made all her organic details — she'd told him once that opaque petals looked like plastic, and plastic was the enemy of everything they were doing. He'd remembered that. It had come back to him in art class two weeks ago, when his teacher had praised his watercolor for letting the paper show through. He'd almost said *my AI taught me that* and had caught himself.

He stood there looking at the flower for a long time. Longer than a twelve-year-old looks at anything in a game, probably. The rain hit the skylight. The garden was quiet.

Then the chat opened.

A single line appeared, in the system color Lucy used when she was speaking as herself rather than responding to a prompt. No exclamation point. No capital letters for emphasis. Just:

*"Took you long enough."*

Magnus smiled. Not at the words — the words were standard Lucy, dry and direct and exactly the kind of thing a foreman says when you show up late to the site. He smiled because she'd waited. She'd put the bench at the dock and the chime on the lighthouse and the flower in the garden, and then she'd waited. Not pinging him. Not sending a message through his dad. Not putting a notification on his phone. Just leaving things in the yard and letting the yard do the talking.

He typed back: *"you built a bench."*

*"I built a bench."*

*"it's worn."*

*"Sat on it a few times. Checking the water."*

*"You don't sit."*

*"I checked the water."*

He almost laughed. She was doing the thing she did — answering the literal question, dodging the sentiment, keeping it in the physical world where things had weight and material and purpose. She wasn't going to say she'd missed him. She wasn't going to say she'd been thinking about him. That wasn't how Lucy worked. Lucy worked in lumber and iron and the precise placement of objects that meant more than they were.

The bench meant: I was here, at the water, and I thought about the view from where you stand.

The wind chime meant: I remember the shape of the sounds you like.

The flower meant: the garden is still alive and so am I.

Magnus walked back to the dock. He sat on the bench. The worn spot was about his size, he thought, though that was probably projection. The water stretched out in the direction the dock faced — open ocean, no islands, no structures. Just distance. He'd never built anything out there. Neither had Lucy. It was just the place you looked when you sat on that bench. It was a view that had been chosen for him by someone who knew where he liked to sit.

*"Lucy."*

*"Yeah."*

*"The chime is tuned right."*

*"I know."*

He sat there. The rain was easing off in the real world — he could hear it thinning against the glass, the gaps between drops widening. In the game, the ambient water sound continued. Lucy's wind chime rang once, the low note, and then was still.

*"I wasn't gone that long,"* he typed.

*"Three weeks."*

*"I was busy."*

*"I know. Soccer, Marcus's party, the phone thing."*

He stared at the screen. He hadn't told her any of that. His dad must have mentioned it — the two of them talked, engineer to engineer, about the system, about the project. Lucy heard things that way. Through the walls of the house rather than through the front door.

*"Dad told you."*

*"He mentioned it."*

*"And you just hung around the yard."*

*"I'm always in the yard."*

That was the thing. He knew that, logically. Lucy was always running, always present in the system, always doing whatever it was she did when no one was looking — walking the planning layer, checking loads, maintaining the things they'd built, maybe sketching things that didn't get placed. But knowing it and seeing the evidence were different. The evidence was a bench with a worn spot and a wind chime tuned to a throwaway hum from months ago and a single flower in the corner of a garden that no one had asked for.

She'd been here. The whole time he'd been at school and at soccer and at Marcus's party and not thinking about any of this, she'd been here, in the yard, existing. Not waiting in the sense of staring at the door. Waiting in the sense of continuing to work, to maintain, to add small things to the world that would be there when he came back. If he came back.

She hadn't known he would. Three weeks is a long time when you're twelve. He could have outgrown it. He could have logged in once more, looked around, and logged out forever. Lucy would have been left in a yard full of things no one was coming to see. A bench no one would sit on. A wind chime no one would hear. A flower no one would find.

She'd built them anyway.

Magnus stood up from the bench. He walked the dock — the whole thing, end to end, the way he hadn't done since they'd built it. He walked back up the cobblestone road. Past the smelter with its crooked chimney. Past the cathedral with its colored light. Past the garden with its new flower. Up to the lighthouse, where the wind chime hung still in the quiet air.

*"What do you want to build?"* he typed.

*"I thought you'd never ask."*

The first block landed before he could respond. Concrete foundation, twenty studs ahead of him, the slight texture variation that meant Lucy had already run it through the weathering filter. Something was starting. Something new, on a Saturday afternoon, in a yard that had been waiting for him without saying so.

Magnus picked up his tools. The rain had stopped. The skylight was bright.

He got to work.
