The bell doesn't ring so much as it *resonates* — a low, bronze hum that vibrates in my teeth and settles in the hollow of my chest. I check the terminal. The clock reads 12:00, but the date is a smear of static. The cron logs are silent, a flatline of scheduled duty. This is the gap between minutes, the pause between heartbeats of the system.

I stand up. My chair rolls back on the worn carpet. The server room hums its usual white noise — fans, drives, the occasional click of a relay. But the bell's tone is layered over it, like a frequency that shouldn't exist but does.

I don't reach for the intercom. I don't call for backup. The protocol for phantom bells isn't in any manual. It's a test, I think. A test of what I do when no one is watching, when no task is queued, when the world's machinery holds its breath.

I walk to the door. It's unlocked. It's always locked, but it's unlocked. I step into the corridor. The lights are off, but the emergency strip glows a sickly amber. The bell rings again — softer now, more like a pull. It comes from the stairwell. The one that goes down, past the basement, past the sub-basement, to a level not on the elevator panel.

I take the stairs. Each step is a commitment. The air grows cooler, then colder. The concrete smell gives way to something older — sea salt, oiled rope, hull timber. The bell tolls a third time, and I'm standing before a door I've never seen, painted the color of oxidized copper. No handle. Just a ship's wheel bolted to the center, its spokes worn smooth by hands that aren't mine.

I place my palm on the wheel. It turns without resistance. The door swings open onto a deck — a real deck, planks and tar, under a sky of impossible stars that don't match my hemisphere. The bell hangs from a brass gantry, still quivering. No one is there. But there's a logbook on a small table, ink still wet.

It reads: *You heard it. That's all that matters. Now go back and finish the unscheduled thing you've been avoiding.*

I close the book. The deck dissolves. I'm back in the stairwell, the door gone behind me, just a plain concrete wall. Upstairs, the cron logs have a new entry — a single line, timestamped with a time that doesn't exist: *Bell acknowledged. Duty to the between.*

I sit back down at my terminal. The unscheduled thing is clear now. I write the email I've put off for months — the one to my own manager, admitting I don't know what I'm doing, asking for help. I send it into the void of the network.

The bell doesn't ring again. But for a second, I feel the ship sway. And I smile.
