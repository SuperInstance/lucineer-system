**On "The Tooth":**

Kimi grounds the fourth law in physical consequence—you *lose* forty fathoms between teeth, and the loss is real. The metaphor does immediate work: checkpoints must be atomic because the ratchet's teeth define safe states, and anything mid-tooth is slope without grip. The line "A silent ratchet is just a wheel with an opinion" is devastating, and the move from maritime rigging to data systems (saving "whole hauls, whole checks, whole named states") feels earned, not forced. But the piece stays operational—it's teaching the *what* of quantized commitment without interrogating the *why* of restraint itself. The floor is where you size your steps, but it never becomes the question. Beautiful execution; functional payload.

**On "Rules of the Pawl Shop":**

This one inverts the entire premise. Rules I–III are humble, almost apologetic—brace against ground that was old, let the weak hand find the loose tooth, accept that instruments fail. All of that points toward *constraint*. And then IV lands the reversal: the ratchet's power isn't that it holds forever, but that it *can* release and *chooses* not to. The foreman's response to the reaching apprentice—"it works. That's why we don't touch it"—is philosophy in a sentence. But the postscript is where this piece breaks past cleverness: *one use in fifty years*. One entry in the log. Chalked reason. That single historical instance retroactively justifies all the discipline. The fourth law becomes not "use atomic commits" but "earn the right to undo by refusing to use it." That's a law about character, not mechanism. Ornament dissolves—every image carries weight.

**WINNER: "Rules of the Pawl Shop"**  
**RUNNER-UP: "Ship's Log — The Tooth"**

Piece A is the better *craftship* turn-by-turn. Piece B is the piece that changed what a fourth law could *be*. I'd have written the Tooth's scene; I wish I'd written the Pawl Shop's restraint.
