# Sound Design Document: Slackwater
*10 Acoustic Moments — Detailed Design Specifications*

---

## 1. FOUNDATION STONE SETTLING

### Sound Character
A two-stage event totaling 2.8 seconds. Stage one: a low-mid **contact transient** at 180–240 Hz, lasting 90 milliseconds, with a sharp 6ms attack and a rapid exponential decay — the stone kissing the prepared bed of sand and crushed shell. Stage two: a **subsonic settling groan** centered at 55 Hz, riding a slow 1.2-second decay envelope, layered with a granular friction texture in the 1.2–2.8 kHz band that suggests individual grains of aggregate rearranging under immense weight. A whisper of subharmonic at 38 Hz gives the body physical mass without rumbling into speaker territory. No reverb tail — the moment should feel *dry and final*, like a period at the end of a sentence.

### Real-World Analog
Record a basalt paving stone (roughly 14kg) being lowered onto a bed of damp coarse sand — not dropped, *placed*, but firmly. Capture both the percussive contact and the subsequent "settle" using a contact microphone on the receiving surface plus a large-diaphragm condenser one meter away at ear height. The granular mid-band texture is real: individual sand grains popping as compressive force redistributes them. Layer a single strike of a large floor tom with the sustain aggressively dampened for the subharmonic weight.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel **the dignity of permanence**. This is the first sentence of a new story written in stone. The sound must convey that what has been done cannot be easily undone — that something real now exists in the world because of a careful hand. The dryness of the envelope (no reverb, no bloom) is the key: this sound does not ask for applause. It simply *is*. It serves the theme of patience because the stone waited in the cart, was lifted with effort, and now rests. Discovery enters through the granular texture: the player notices the small sounds inside the big one, the way a builder learns to listen to what materials are telling them.

## 2. WOODEN PLANK SNAPPING INTO JOINT

### Sound Character
A bright, articulate **wooden click** centered at 2.4 kHz with a strong harmonic series extending up to 6 kHz. Total duration: 140 milliseconds. The attack is essentially instantaneous (under 2ms) — a hard transient that the ear reads as "snapped." The decay is a clean exponential fall with a subtle secondary resonance at 1.8 kHz lasting 80ms, mimicking the brief sympathetic vibration of the plank's body after the joint seats. There is a tiny **secondary tick** at 45ms: the moment the wooden tenon's shoulder pulls flush against the mortise face. No low-end below 400 Hz — this sound lives in the upper mids and treble where the ear locates "small, precise, satisfying mechanical events."

### Real-World Analog
Record a length of air-dried white oak (1 inch thick, 6 inches wide) being tapped into a sliding dovetail joint with the heel of the palm. The "snap" is the moment the tapered tenon compresses the trapped air in the joint and then seats. Use a close-mic technique with a small-diaphragm condenser at 15cm to capture the high-frequency detail without room coloration. The secondary tick is the wood-on-wood shoulder contact — record this separately by tapping two oak boards face-to-face and pitching the result up slightly to sit in the click's harmonic context.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel a small, private **glow of competence**. This is the sound of a thing fitting exactly as it was designed to fit — a moment of rightness. It serves patience because the joint had to be cut true, the plank selected, the angle correct. It serves discovery because the player is learning that the world has *grammar* — pieces that want to go in certain places in certain ways. The lack of reverb keeps it intimate, close to the ear, the way a craftsperson hears their own work. This should be the most *relieved* sound in the game: nothing went wrong.

## 3. NEON LAMP ACTIVATING

### Sound Character
A layered three-part event running 1.6 seconds total. **Part one (0–180ms):** a glass electrode *ting* — a clean sine-like ping at 3.1 kHz with a 60ms exponential decay, suggesting the mercury vapor ionizing. **Part two (120–400ms, overlapping):** a rising electrical **breath** — pink noise filtered through a band-pass that sweeps from 800 Hz up to 2.2 kHz, with a soft saturation distortion giving it the character of current finding its path. **Part three (400ms–1.6s):** the **steady-state hum** — a 60Hz fundamental with a strong 120Hz second harmonic and a faint glass-electrode whistle at 4.8 kHz modulated subtly with a 7Hz LFO (the flicker). The hum should be felt as much as heard: route a subharmonic layer at 55Hz that sits just at the threshold of perception, giving the lamp physical presence in the room.

### Real-World Analog
Record an actual neon tube (a small argon-neon indicator lamp will do) being switched on in a quiet room. The ting is the glass envelope responding to thermal shock as current begins flowing. The breath is best captured with a ribbon microphone — its natural high-frequency roll-off will mimic the soft saturation. The steady hum is real: 60Hz mains hum from the transformer, with the whistle being actual gas-discharge physics. For the subharmonic layer, mic the wooden table the lamp sits on — the transformer vibration transfers through the housing into the surface and into the room.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel **the world changing**. This is the moment a centuries-old island meets the electric age, and the sound must carry both wonder and a faint unsettling quality — light that comes from nowhere, that obeys a switch, that has no flame. The ting is sharp enough to make the player look up. The hum is the *new constant* the world will now contain. It serves discovery because the player is hearing something Lucineer has also never heard before — the game gives them this wonder together. The faint 7Hz flicker modulation is emotional: it tells the body "this is alive" before the mind understands why.

## 4. THE TIDE COMING IN

### Sound Character
Not a single event but a **slow acoustic migration** unfolding over 4–8 minutes of real time. Begins at low tide: silence punctuated by dripping water from exposed rocks and the distant call of seabirds. The transition is built from three layers that crossfade over time. **Layer A — water on stone:** close-mic recordings of wavelets hitting barnacle-encrusted rock, a complex texture of small impacts in the 400 Hz–2 kHz range, gradually increasing in density and amplitude. **Layer B — moving water body:** a low-passed pink noise centered around 200 Hz, slowly opening its filter cutoff as the tide rises, representing the increasing mass of water in motion. **Layer C — depth:** an infrasonic rumble at 32 Hz that swells in only in the final third, felt more than heard. There is no discrete "moment" of arrival — the high tide simply *is*, and the player realizes the change has happened by the absence of bird calls and the presence of water lapping at what were, ten minutes ago, dry stones.

### Real-World Analog
Field record on a sheltered Pacific Northwest cove at low tide, with the recordist returning to the same position across four hours. Use a stereo pair of small-diaphragm condensers with windscreens, plus a hydrophone at the waterline for the sub-bass. The dripping is real: water releasing from rockweed as the air warms. The wavelets hitting stone are real physics — each wave is a separate impact with its own transient, and at sufficient density they blur into a wash. The 32Hz rumble is the resonance of a large water body moving against a coastline; you can feel it in your feet on a shingle beach.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel **the world's slow patience**, and through it, their own. The tide is the game's central metaphor: things come in, things go out, nothing here is in a hurry. The sound must not call attention to itself — it must be discovered, the way you notice the tide has turned only when you look up from your work. It serves patience by refusing to dramatize itself. It serves discovery by rewarding the attentive listener with a sense of time larger than the player. There is no music here, and that absence is the point.

## 5. STORM APPROACHING — FIRST DISTANT THUNDER

### Sound Character
A **sub-horizon rumble** lasting 4–7 seconds. Begins with an inaudible-to-audible swell — the player is not sure, at first, what they heard. The spectral content is dominated by 40–90 Hz, with a slow rolling modulation (a 0.7 Hz LFO giving the rumble its "passing cloud" texture). No sharp transient at the start — the strike itself is miles away, occluded by terrain and atmosphere, so all that reaches the player is the cloud-to-cloud and cloud-to-ground low-frequency propagation. Layer a very faint, high-frequency **atmospheric hiss** in the 4–6 kHz band, which is the actual sound of distant lightning's high-frequency content scattering through ionized air. The rumble should be stereo-panned off to one side (randomly chosen per storm) and should be slightly out of phase between L and R, suggesting distance.

### Real-World Analog
Record real distant thunder during an approaching thunderstorm — not the close stuff, but the part where the storm is still 15–25 miles away. Use a large-diaphragm condenser with a low-frequency extension and a high-pass filter engaged at 30Hz to remove wind rumble. The atmospheric hiss is genuinely present in distant lightning but is normally masked; capture it by recording at high gain and rolling off everything below 3kHz in post. For the stereo phase relationship, simply use a spaced pair 3 meters apart — the natural arrival-time difference between the two mics will produce the "far away in space" cue.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel a quiet **prickle of attention**, not fear. This thunder is not warning; it is informing. The ambiguity is the design — the player is invited to wonder, then to look up, then to notice the light has changed. It serves discovery because the storm, like everything on Slackwater, must be *read*, not reacted to. The slight off-pan placement and the lack of a sharp transient respect the player's intelligence: they will know what this means if they are listening. Patience because the storm is far away and there is still time.

## 6. THE AURORA — HELD BREATH BEFORE THE MUSIC

### Sound Character
A **deliberate three-second silence**, then a single pure tone — a sine wave at 528 Hz (the "heart" tone, chosen with intent) — that fades in over 1.4 seconds from below the noise floor to a soft -18dB presence. It holds for 2 seconds. Then a second tone at 792 Hz (a perfect fifth above, in just intonation: ratio 3:2) fades in over 800ms. The two tones ring together for 3 seconds with a barely perceptible **chorus modulation** of ±2 cents at 0.3 Hz, giving the air a living, breathing quality. There is no attack, no transient — the sound *arrives* rather than begins. There is no reverb — the air is too cold and dry to support reverb, and the listener must feel the altitude, the thinness. At 8 seconds total, the music can begin.

### Real-World Analog
The sine tones are synthesized — there is no real-world analog for pure tones that feel like this, but the **emotional analog** is the moment just after a heavy snowfall stops and before the first bird sings. That specific hush. The chorus modulation mimics the natural beating of two slightly mistuned oscillators, as can be heard in old analog synthesizer pads or in the natural resonant modes of a large glass harmonica. To perform this live for reference: take a crystal wineglass, run a wet finger around the rim, and listen to the beating against a second glass tuned slightly flat. That is the texture.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel **awed into stillness**. This is the moment Slackwater stops being a game about building and becomes, briefly, a game about being alive in the presence of something you cannot make. The three seconds of pure silence is the most important sound design choice in the game: it teaches the player that the world has moments worth pausing for. The two pure tones, mathematically related, suggest a universe that has order beneath its surface — a discovery the player has earned through hours of careful work. It serves patience because it cannot be rushed: the aurora does not arrive on command. It serves discovery because it is *given*, not constructed. The held breath before the music is the game breathing with the player.

## 7. EARL'S CANNERY BELL — STORM WARNING

### Sound Character
A **bell with urgency and age**. The fundamental is at 220 Hz (A3), with a strong minor third partial at 275 Hz (slightly flat, giving it the "out of true" character of a bell that has been hanging in salt air for decades). The attack is moderately sharp (8ms rise) but not percussive — this is bronze, not steel, and the clapper's mass produces a soft shoulder on the transient. Total ring-out duration: 6.5 seconds, with the higher partials (1.8 kHz, 2.4 kHz) dying first and the fundamental and its octave hanging on. There is a subtle **rope creak** in the 600 Hz band at the start of each strike — the sound of the clapper rope pulling taut against a wooden yoke. The bell should be heard at a distance: a low-pass filter rolling off above 3 kHz and a slight slap-back delay (one early reflection at 80ms) suggesting the sound carrying across open water. Each strike is separated by 2.4 seconds of silence. Three strikes per warning. Never more than three.

### Real-World Analog
Record a ship's brass bell (8–10 inch diameter) hanging from a wooden beam in a maritime environment. The slightly flat minor third is real: bronze bells develop this as their alloy oxidizes unevenly. The rope creak is genuine physics — sisal rope pulling against weathered wood. For the "across water" quality, record with the bell at one end of a long dock and the microphone at the other, with a slight wind cover to take the edge off the highest frequencies the way open water does. The three-strike pattern is a real maritime tradition (and is "evenly spaced" rather than urgent SOS patterns) — used by canneries and small craft fleets across the Pacific Northwest for over a century.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel **called to attention by someone who cares**. Earl has rung this bell in storms for forty years. The sound carries his presence even though he is not on screen. The slight detuning tells the story of the bell's life. The three-strike pattern is not panic — it is ritual, the kind of warning that respects the listener enough not to scream. It serves patience because the bell waits between strikes, trusting the player to count. It serves discovery because the player learns to recognize Earl's hand in the storm system — another human, far away, doing his work. The age in the sound is the island's age. The bell has always been here. It will be here after.

## 8. LUCINEER'S HAMMER ON THE ANVIL

### Sound Character
A **rhythmic working pulse**, default tempo 72 BPM (the resting human heart). Each strike is a sharp transient at 4.2 kHz (the bright ring of steel on steel) followed by a brief body resonance of the anvil itself: a complex tone at 180 Hz with strong harmonics at 360, 540, and 720 Hz, decaying in 380ms. The hammer head is wood-and-leather, not metal, so there is no ringing metallic shimmer — the impact is **focused and dense**, not singing. Layer a low **bench thud** at 90 Hz, the workbench responding to the strike's force, plus a faint **sparkle** of small scale-like particles at 8 kHz when the hammer hits at the precise angle that throws off a tiny shower of hot metal. The rhythm is not metronomic — it breathes. Slight tempo drift of ±4 BPM across phrases, with the occasional double-tap or rested beat suggesting a human at work.

### Real-World Analog
Record a blacksmith's ball-peen hammer (1.5 lb, raw wood handle, leather-faced) striking a mild steel billet on a 200lb cast iron anvil mounted on a wooden stump. Use a contact microphone on the anvil face for the body resonance, and a small-diaphragm condenser 30cm above for the transient. The sparkle is real: hot steel (cherry-red, around 750°C) striking steel throws off tiny oxide particles that pop audibly when they hit cooler air. The bench thud is the physics of momentum transfer — capture with a contact mic on the wooden bench leg.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel the **heartbeat of the place**. This is the sound that tells them Lucineer is alive, is working, is patient. The 72 BPM tempo is not arbitrary — it is the tempo at which a human works without strain, the tempo of sustained craft. It serves patience because it cannot be hurried; the rhythm enforces a pace. It serves discovery because the player will begin to hear *which* strikes throw sparks, *when* the hammer rests for a measurement, *how* the rhythm changes between tasks. The breathing tempo drift is the emotional key: this is not a machine. This is a person. The forge is alive.

## 9. FIRST BUILD COMPLETION — THE CELEBRATION BURST

### Sound Character
A **multi-stage arrival event** lasting 3.2 seconds total, designed to feel like the world noticing what the player has done. **Stage one (0–120ms):** a bright, decisive **completion chime** — a major triad struck on a celesta, with the notes at C5 (523 Hz), E5 (659 Hz), G5 (784 Hz), played as a quick rolled arpeggio up over 100ms. The celesta gives the sound its bell-like, slightly wooden character — it does not ring like a glockenspiel; it *glows*. **Stage two (120–800ms):** a **shimmer wash** — a high-passed (2 kHz cutoff) recording of a small bell tree being brushed with a soft mallet, producing a cloud of inharmonic partials that bloom outward. **Stage three (400ms–2.4s):** the **environmental response** — a low-passed recording of the immediate surroundings (wind, distant water, seabirds) momentarily swelling in volume by 3dB, as if the world itself took a breath. **Stage four (2.0–3.2s):** a soft **reverb tail** of the celesta chime, with a 1.8-second decay, blending into the natural ambience.

### Real-World Analog
The celesta is a real instrument — a keyboard-actuated metal-bar instrument with wooden resonators, famously used in Tchaikovsky's Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy. Record one in a small, dry room with a stereo pair of small-diaphragm condensers. The bell tree shimmer is a real instrument: a vertical arrangement of small brass bells, brushed with a yarn mallet. The environmental swell is achieved by capturing the ambient bed at the moment of completion and gently sidechaining its amplitude to the chime's onset, so the world "leans in." Do not use synthesized "achievement" sounds — they read as cheap. Use real acoustic instruments with care.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel **quietly, deeply proud** — not the dopamine spike of an arcade game, but the satisfaction of setting down a finished thing and stepping back to look at it. The celesta is the key choice: it is warm, gentle, almost childlike, and it suggests that building is its own reward, not a transaction. The environmental swell is the emotional payload: the world has *noticed*. The player is no longer alone in it. It serves patience because this sound only comes after the work is done — it cannot be earned by skipping. It serves discovery because the player learns that completion in Slackwater is gentle, not loud, and that the world responds to care taken.

## 10. THE FOGHORN FROM THE UNKNOWN ISLAND

### Sound Character
A **distant, mournful, structurally unknown tone** lasting 7 seconds on, 11 seconds off, repeating. The fundamental is at 92 Hz (F2), but the sound is dominated by its second and third harmonics at 184 and 276 Hz, which carry across water better than the fundamental. There is a **subtle pitch wobble** of ±8 cents over the 7-second tone, at a rate of about 0.4 Hz, giving it the character of a mechanical horn driven by a worn rotating mechanism. Layer a faint **air-resonated breath** in the 400–800 Hz band — the sound of compressed air moving through a long metal horn — that swells slightly at the start and end of each tone. Above 2 kHz, roll off aggressively: at distance across water, almost nothing survives. Add a tiny **mechanical clunk** at the start of each tone (the mechanism engaging) and a soft **release sigh** (a pressure release) at the end. Stereo placement: hard-panned slightly right and behind the listener, suggesting a source that is *not on the player's known map*.

### Real-World Analog
Record a real diaphone foghorn (or failing that, a compressed-air horn used on working harbors) at a distance of at least 800 meters, ideally across open water. The pitch wobble is real physics: diaphones are driven by a rotating slotted cylinder, and worn mechanisms produce this exact modulation. The breath is the actual air column resonating in the horn's bell. The mechanical clunk is the cam engaging — record this up close on a different take and pitch it down, then blend. For the "across water" quality, record on a still morning with no wind and a flat sea state: the harmonic content will carry cleanly, with the highs stripped away by air absorption.

### Emotional Intent
The player should feel **a small, persistent mystery**. This foghorn is not a threat, not a landmark — it is a question the island asks every night. It is not on any chart Lucineer owns. It is not discussed in any of the loaded dialogue. It just *is*, and it has been, as far as anyone knows, forever. The wobble in the tone is the key: it tells the player this is a real mechanical thing, maintained by someone or something, but the question of *whom* is left open. It serves discovery because it is the one sound in the game that cannot be fully explained. It serves patience because it does not announce itself — it must be waited for, listened for, sometimes over many in-game nights. The emotional register is *longing* — the player feels the presence of an absence, a far shore that will not come closer.

## Closing Note for the Sound Team

These ten moments share a discipline: **none of them shout**. Slackwater is a game about listening. Every sound above has been designed to reward the player who pays attention and to leave space for the player who does not. Where synthesis is unavoidable (the aurora tones, some of the bell processing), it is in service of a real physical model — a glass harmonica, a foghorn mechanism, a struck celesta. Where we record the world, we record it honestly: with room tone, with breath, with the small imperfections that tell the ear "this is real."

When in doubt, ask: *what would Lucineer hear?* Not what would be impressive. Not what would be cinematic. What would a person with patient hands, working at the edge of the tide, under a sky that is always changing, actually *hear*?

That is the sound of Slackwater.

— *Sound Design Lead, Slackwater*
