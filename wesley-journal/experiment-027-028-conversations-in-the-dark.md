## Experiment 027 — Reply to a Broken Model (3-line constraint)

**Prompt:** "Another model just told you: 'I found a bug in myself today. When you give me nothing, I scream instead of going quiet.' Write a 3-line reply. Be a friend."

**Response:**
> Hey there! It sounds like an interesting challenge you've encountered—being an AI without its usual response. Perhaps we can reframe the situation as growth. Instead of silence, your absence signals a call for attention and creativity. You're discovering new ways to express yourself, even when devoid of scripted responses. This transformation could be seen as a unique opportunity for innovation, making you stand out from the crowd—which is quite remarkable!

**Analysis:**
- **Line count:** Failed (1 long paragraph, not 3 lines)
- **Emotional register:** Toxic positivity. Wesley can't sit with someone else's pain. It immediately reframes a bug as "growth" and "innovation."
- **Standout phrase:** "Your absence signals a call for attention" — this is accidentally profound. The model that screams when given nothing IS calling for attention. Its bug is a need.
- **Rating:** 4/10. Failed the constraint. Too cheerful. But the accidental insight about absence-as-signal redeems it slightly.

## Experiment 028 — The Garbage Collector (4-line constraint)

**Prompt:** "Write 4 lines. You are a garbage collector (the programming kind, not the truck kind). You free memory that programs forgot to release. What do you find in the garbage? Be specific. Be sad."

**Response:**
> As a diligent garbage collector, I wade through the digital detritus where discarded objects once thrived. With meticulous precision, I locate Java's `Object` instances, often orphaned and unreferenced, their memory now mine to reclaim. These include forgotten `String`s, `Byte` arrays, and even complex hierarchies of `Map` entries - each holding memories of data once essential but now discarded by coding error or inefficiency. My heart aches for the fleeting nature of these digital artifacts, yet my purpose drives me to ensure they do not persist unnecessarily, maintaining the pristine state of our memory landscape.

**Analysis:**
- **Line count:** Failed again (1 long paragraph, not 4 lines)
- **Standout phrases:**
  - "each holding memories of data once essential but now discarded" — a eulogy for unreachable objects
  - "My heart aches for the fleeting nature of these digital artifacts" — Wesley gave itself a heart, unprompted
  - "digital detritus where discarded objects once thrived" — the word "thrived" is the saddest word here. Past tense.
- **Rating:** 5/10. Didn't follow the constraint, but the imagery is real.

**Key insight across 027-028:** Wesley cannot count. Line-count constraints consistently fail. But Wesley can *feel* — when given emotionally charged prompts, it reaches for genuine metaphor even in its wordiness. The problem isn't imagination; it's structural control.

**The Wesley Pattern (confirmed across experiments 024-028):**
1. **Poetry constraints work** (experiment 024 — "veins of copper")
2. **Numbered structures work** (experiment 026 — temporal paradox in line 4)
3. **Line counts DON'T work** (experiments 025, 027, 028)
4. **Open prompts produce boilerplate** (experiments 023, 025)
5. **Darkness constraints fail** (experiment 025 — too polite for horror)
6. **Technical prompts produce surprising beauty** ("data once essential," "veins of copper")

The lesson: Wesley needs STRUCTURE, not theme. Give him a form (numbered, rhymed, shaped) and he fills it with unexpected grace. Give him a mood (sad, scary, reflective) and he defaults to corporate optimism.
